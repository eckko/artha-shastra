08.01.1 | When calamities (vyasana) occur simultaneously, whether one is to march or to protect, according to ease — this is the consideration of calamities.
08.01.2 | A calamity of a constituent element, divine or human, arises from misfortune and from bad policy.
08.01.3 | A calamity is the reversal of excellences, absence, a grave defect, excessive attachment, or affliction.
08.01.4 | It casts him away from well-being — hence it is called a calamity (vyasana).
08.01.5 | 'Among the calamities of the king, the minister, the country, the fort, the treasury, the army and the ally, each preceding one is more serious,' say the teachers.
08.01.6 | 'No,' says Bharadvaja.
08.01.7 | 'Between the calamities of the king and the minister, the calamity of the minister is more serious.
08.01.8 | Counsel, the attainment of the fruit of counsel, the carrying out of works, the business of income and expenditure, the administration of punishment, the warding off of enemies and forest-tribes, the protection of the kingdom, the remedying of calamities, the protection of the princes and the consecration of the princes depend on the ministers.
08.01.9 | In their absence those (functions) are absent, and, as with one whose wings are cut, there is loss of activity for the king.
08.01.10 | And in their calamities, secret instigation by the enemy is near at hand.
08.01.11 | And when they become defective, there is danger to the king's life, because they move close to his life (person).'
08.01.12 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.01.13 | It is the king alone who makes the body of servants headed by the councillor and the chaplain, the activity of the superintendents, the remedying of the calamities of the human and material constituent elements, and their prospering.
08.01.14 | Or, when the ministers are afflicted by calamity, he makes others, free from calamity, (ministers).
08.01.15 | He remains ever intent on honouring those who deserve honour and on restraining the treasonable.
08.01.16 | And the king, endowed with excellences, makes the constituent elements excellent by his own excellences.
08.01.17 | Of whatever character he is, of that character the constituent elements become, because in exertion and in negligence they are dependent on him.
08.01.18 | For the king stands in the position of their summit (head).
08.01.19 | 'Between the calamities of the minister and the country, the calamity of the country is more serious,' says Vishalaksha.
08.01.20 | 'The treasury, the army, raw materials (kupya), forced labour, conveyances and stores arise from the country.
08.01.21 | In the absence of the country these are absent, and (the absence) of the king and the minister follows immediately.'
08.01.22 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.01.23 | All undertakings have the ministers as their root: the successes of the country's works, the securing of acquisition and security from one's own side and from the other's, the remedying of calamities, the settling of empty land and its growth, and favour in the matter of fines and taxes.
08.01.24 | 'Between the calamities of the country and the fort, the calamity of the fort (is more serious),' say the Parasharas.
08.01.25 | 'For in the fort lies the origin of the treasury and the army, and (it is) a refuge for the country in adversity.
08.01.26 | And the townsmen are more powerful than the country people, and are constant helpers of the king in adversity.
08.01.27 | But the country people are shared with the enemy (too).'
08.01.28 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.01.29 | The undertakings of the fort, the treasury, the army, embankments (irrigation works) and vārttā (economic activity) have the country as their root.
08.01.30 | Bravery, steadfastness, skill and abundance (of numbers) are found in the country people.
08.01.31 | And forts on mountains and river-islands are not inhabited in the absence of the country.
08.01.32 | But in a country consisting mostly of cultivators, the calamity of the fort (is more serious); in a country consisting mostly of soldiers, the calamity of the country (is more serious).
08.01.33 | “Of the calamities of the fort and the treasury, that of the treasury (is more serious),” says Pishuna.
08.01.34 | “For the building up of the fort, the guarding of the fort, keeping in check the country, the ally and the enemy, the inciting of those in distant lands, and the employment of the forces of the army have the treasury as their root.
08.01.35 | The fort can be won over by secret instigation by means of the enemies' treasury.
08.01.36 | And in a calamity it is possible to go away taking the treasury, but not the fort.”
08.01.37 | “No,” says Kautilya.
08.01.38 | The treasury, the army, silent war, keeping one's own party in check, the employment of the forces of the army, the receiving of a reinforcer, and the warding off of the enemy's army and forest tribes rest on the fort.
08.01.39 | And in the absence of a fort, the treasury becomes the enemies'.
08.01.40 | For it is seen that those who possess forts are not exterminated.
08.01.41 | “Of the calamities of the treasury and the army, that of the army (is more serious),” says Kaunapadanta.
08.01.42 | “For keeping the ally and the enemy in check, the inciting of the enemy's army, and the holding together of one's own army have the army as their root.
08.01.43 | And in the absence of an army, the destruction of the treasury is certain.
08.01.44 | And in the absence of a treasury the army can be held together by means of raw materials, land, or the seizing of the enemy's land for oneself; and by one who possesses an army a treasury can be gathered.
08.01.45 | And because of its close attendance on the king, the army shares the nature of the minister.”
08.01.46 | “No,” says Kautilya.
08.01.47 | For the army has the treasury as its root.
08.01.48 | In the absence of a treasury the army goes over to the enemy, or kills the king.
08.01.49 | And the treasury brings about every undertaking and is the means of dharma and pleasure.
08.01.50 | But according to place, time and task, one or the other of the treasury and the army becomes the deciding factor.
08.01.51 | For the army serves for the acquisition and protection of the treasury, while the treasury serves for that of both the treasury and the army.
08.01.52 | Because it sets all things in motion, the calamity of the treasury is the more serious.
08.01.53 | “Of the calamities of the army and the ally, that of the ally (is more serious),” says Vatavyadhi.
08.01.54 | “The ally, unpaid and at a distance, does work; it counteracts the heel-seizer, the reinforcer, the enemy and the forest chief; and when a state of calamity arises it helps with treasury, army and land.”
08.01.55 | “No,” says Kautilya.
08.01.56 | For one who possesses an army the ally remains in the condition of an ally, or the enemy comes to be in the condition of an ally.
08.01.57 | But between the army and the ally, in a task common to both, the distinction is in point of strength and from the advantage of place and time for one's own fighting.
08.01.58 | But in a swift expedition, and in an uprising of an enemy or forest chief who is not in the interior, no ally is found.
08.01.59 | When calamities occur simultaneously and when the enemy grows, the ally stands firm where there is a connection with gain.
08.01.60 | Thus the deliberation on the calamities of the constituent elements has been stated.
08.01.61a | But as regards the calamity of the parts of a constituent element in particular, it is numerousness, or loyalty, or strength that accomplishes the task.
08.01.61b | ~
08.01.62a | But when the calamity of two is equal, the distinction is according to the loss of qualities, provided that the possession of good qualities by the remaining constituent elements would not be unamenable.
08.01.62b | ~
08.01.63a | But where the destruction of the remaining constituent elements would result from the calamity of one, that calamity would be the graver, whether it be of the principal one or of another.

08.02.1 | The king and the kingdom: this is the sum of the constituent elements.
08.02.2 | A revolt (kopa) against the king is either internal or external: this is the topic.
08.02.3 | Like the danger from a snake, internal revolt is worse than external revolt; and revolt of the ministers within is worse than internal revolt.
08.02.4 | Therefore he should keep the power of treasury and army under his own control.
08.02.5 | "Of dual rule (dvairājya) and foreign rule (vairājya), dual rule perishes through hatred and partiality towards each other's party, or through mutual rivalry; foreign rule, however, looking to the winning of the hearts of the constituent elements, is enjoyed by others as it stands," say the teachers.
08.02.6 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.02.7 | Dual rule of father and son or of two brothers, with equal acquisition and security and with restraint by the ministers, carries on.
08.02.8 | Foreign rule, however: having wrested (the kingdom) from another who is alive, thinking "this is not mine", he impoverishes it, carries (its wealth) away, or makes it an article of sale, or, when it has become disaffected, abandons it and goes away.
08.02.9 | "A blind king or one deviating from the science (śāstra)": "The blind one, lacking the eye of the science, doing whatever comes to him, firmly obstinate or led by others, destroys the kingdom by injustice; the one deviating from the science, however, in whatever matter his mind deviates from the science, there becomes amenable to persuasion," say the teachers.
08.02.10 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.02.11 | A blind king can be set right in any matter whatever through the excellence of his helpers.
08.02.12 | But the one deviating from the science, his mind set otherwise than the science, destroys by injustice both the kingdom and himself.
08.02.13 | "A sick king or a new king": "A sick king incurs injury to the kingdom rooted in the ministers, or affliction of his life rooted in the kingdom; a new king, however, acts with acts of favour, exemption, gift and honour belonging to his own dharma, and with benefits that please the constituent elements," say the teachers.
08.02.14 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.02.15 | A sick king keeps the royal administration going as it has been carried on.
08.02.16 | A new king, however, thinking "this kingdom is mine", treats (the kingdom) won by force as he pleases, without restraint.
08.02.17 | Or, held in check by those who rose up with him, he tolerates injury to the kingdom.
08.02.18 | Not rooted among the constituent elements, he is easy to uproot.
08.02.19 | In the case of the sick one there is a distinction: one with a malignant disease and one without a malignant disease.
08.02.20 | In the case of the new one too: well-born and not well-born.
08.02.21 | "A weak king of high birth or a strong king of low birth": "The constituent elements, having regard to weakness, go along with the secret instigation (upajāpa) of a weak king of high birth only with difficulty, but easily with that of a strong king of low birth, having regard to strength," say the teachers.
08.02.22 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.02.23 | The constituent elements of their own accord submit to a weak king of high birth; sovereignty by its nature follows the well-born one.
08.02.24 | And they frustrate the secret instigation of a strong king of low birth; in affection lies the totality of all qualities.
08.02.25 | Because it destroys the effort (spent on it), destruction of the crop is worse than destruction of the handful; because it leaves no livelihood, drought is worse than excessive rain.
08.02.26a | For each pair of calamities, the strength and weakness of the constituent elements has been stated in order of succession; it is the reason for marching and for staying.

08.03.1 | Lack of training in the sciences is the cause of a man's vices (vyasana).
08.03.2 | For an untrained man does not see the evils of the vices.
08.03.3 | We shall set them forth.
08.03.4 | The set born of anger is threefold; the set born of desire is fourfold.
08.03.5 | Of the two, anger is the more serious.
08.03.6 | For anger operates everywhere.
08.03.7 | And it is mostly heard that kings under the sway of anger were killed by uprisings of the constituents (prakṛti), and those under the sway of desire, on account of their decline, by enemies and diseases.
08.03.8 | "No," says Bharadvaja.
08.03.9 | "Anger is the conduct of good men; it is the requital of enmity, the destruction of contempt, and a state in which men are afraid.
08.03.10 | And constant association with anger serves the purpose of preventing evil.
08.03.11 | Desire is the winning of success, conciliation, a disposition to generosity, and being well-loved.
08.03.12 | And constant association with desire serves the enjoyment of the fruit of action done."
08.03.13 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.03.14 | Anger means becoming hateful, acquiring enemies, and attachment to sorrow.
08.03.15 | Desire means contempt, loss of wealth, and association with worthless persons: thieves, gamblers, hunters, singers and musicians.
08.03.16 | Of the two, becoming hateful is more serious than contempt.
08.03.17 | One who is held in contempt is held in check by his own people and by others; one who is hated is destroyed.
08.03.18 | Acquiring enemies is more serious than loss of wealth.
08.03.19 | Loss of wealth harms the treasury; acquiring enemies harms life.
08.03.20 | Association with sorrow is more serious than association with the worthless.
08.03.21 | Association with the worthless can be remedied in a moment, but attachment to sorrows causes prolonged distress.
08.03.22 | Therefore anger is the more serious.
08.03.23 | Harshness of speech, injury to property, and harshness of punishment: these (are the three born of anger).
08.03.24 | "Of harshness of speech and injury to property, harshness of speech is the more serious," says Vishalaksha.
08.03.25 | "For a spirited man at whom harsh words have been hurled rises up in return with his spirit.
08.03.26 | The dart of an ill-spoken word, lodged in the heart, inflames the spirit and torments the senses."
08.03.27 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.03.28 | Honouring with wealth removes the dart of speech, but injury to property means loss of livelihood.
08.03.29 | Not giving, taking away, destroying, or giving up wealth: this is injury to property.
08.03.30 | "Of injury to property and harshness of punishment, injury to property is the more serious," say the Parasharas.
08.03.31 | "Dharma and desire have wealth (artha) as their root.
08.03.32 | And the world goes on bound up with wealth.
08.03.33 | Injury to it is the more serious."
08.03.34 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.03.35 | Even for a very great gain, no one would wish for the destruction of his body.
08.03.36 | And through harshness of punishment he incurs that very evil from others.
08.03.37 | Thus the set of three born of anger.
08.03.38 | The one born of desire, on the other hand, is the set of four: hunting, gambling, women and drink.
08.03.39 | Of that, "between hunting and gambling, hunting is the more serious," says Pishuna.
08.03.40 | "In it there is danger to life: fear of robbers, enemies, wild beasts, forest fire and stumbling, loss of direction, and hunger and thirst.
08.03.41 | In gambling, on the other hand, one who knows dice simply wins, as (was won) by Jayatsena and Duryodhana." So it is said.
08.03.42 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.03.43 | Even of those two there is the defeat of one or the other; that is illustrated by Nala and Yudhishthira.
08.03.44 | That very property won (at play) is a bait, and (there is) a continuation of enmity.
08.03.45 | Loss of wealth one has, acquisition of wealth that does not exist, loss of wealth before it is enjoyed, and contracting disease through holding urine and faeces, hunger and the like: these are the evils of gambling.
08.03.46 | In hunting, on the other hand, there is exercise, removal of phlegm, bile, fat and sweat, practice at the target on a moving and on a standing body, knowledge of the minds of animals in situations of anger and fear, and marching that is not constant. So (runs the view).
08.03.47 | "Between the vices of gambling and women, the vice of gambling (is the more serious)," says Kaunapadanta.
08.03.48 | "For the gambler keeps gambling continuously, at night by lamplight, and even when his mother has died;
08.03.49 | and when questioned in a difficulty, he gets angry.
08.03.50 | In the vice of women, on the other hand, inquiry into dharma and wealth does take place at the places of bathing, toilet and meals.
08.03.51 | And a woman can be employed in the king's interest, or be turned away or got rid of by secret punishment or by disease." So it is said.
08.03.52 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.03.53 | Gambling has something that can be won back; the vice of women has nothing that can be won back.
08.03.54 | Not appearing (in public), aversion to work, harm and loss of dharma through letting time pass, weakness of the administration, and addiction to drink: these (are the evils of the vice of women).
08.03.55 | "Between the vices of women and drink, the vice of women (is the more serious)," says Vatavyadhi.
08.03.56 | "For folly of many kinds in regard to women has been explained in 'Conduct in the inner apartments' (niśānta-praṇidhi).
08.03.57 | In drinking, on the other hand, there is enjoyment of the sense-objects, sound and the rest, giving out of affection, honouring of attendants, and removal of the fatigue of work." So it is said.
08.03.58 | "No," says Kautilya.
08.03.59 | In the vice of women there is birth of offspring and protection of oneself in the case of wives within (the household); the opposite in the case of outside women; and complete ruin in the case of those who must not be approached.
08.03.60 | Both of those are (found) in the vice of drink.
08.03.61 | The excellence of drink (is): loss of consciousness, madness of one who is not mad, the state of a corpse of one who is not dead, exposure of the private parts, loss of learning, intelligence, life, wealth and friends, separation from the good, association with the worthless, and addiction to skills of lute and song, which destroy wealth.
08.03.62 | Between gambling and liquor, gambling (is the more serious).
08.03.63 | The victory or defeat of one side, for the sake of a stake, whether (in play) with living creatures or with inanimate things, causes anger among the constituent elements through the division into two parties.
08.03.64 | And especially among confederacies and among royal families that have the nature of a confederacy, there is division caused by gambling, and destruction caused by that; therefore favouring of the bad is the most sinful of vices, because of (its causing) weakness of the administration. So (ends the argument).
08.03.65a | Desire is the favouring of the bad, and anger is the restraining of the good; because of the abundance of their evils both are considered extreme vices.
08.03.65b | ~
08.03.66a | Therefore the self-possessed one, who attends on the elders and has conquered his senses, should give up anger and desire, the starting-point of vices, which destroy the root.

08.04.1 | Affliction from the divine: fire, water, disease, famine and pestilence.
08.04.2 | 'Of fire and water, affliction by fire is irremediable and burns everything, while affliction by water can be escaped from and its harm can be crossed over,' say the teachers.
08.04.3 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.4 | Fire burns a village or half a village, but a flood of water sweeps away a hundred villages.
08.04.5 | 'Of disease and famine, disease ruins works through the stoppage of the activity of the dead, the sick, those stricken and those attending on them; famine, on the other hand, does not ruin works and yields gold, cattle and taxes,' say the teachers.
08.04.6 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.7 | Disease afflicts one region and can be remedied; famine afflicts the whole country and leads to the loss of life of living beings.
08.04.8 | By that, pestilence is explained.
08.04.9 | 'Of the loss of lesser men and the loss of chiefs, the loss of lesser men brings about lack of acquisition and security for works; the loss of chiefs has the nature of obstructing the carrying out of works,' say the teachers.
08.04.10 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.11 | The loss of lesser men can be made good, because lesser men are many; not the loss of chiefs.
08.04.12 | For among thousands there is one chief, or not even one, because of his superiority in spirit and intelligence and because the lesser men depend on him.
08.04.13 | 'Of one's own troops and the enemy's troops, one's own troops oppress with excessive fines and taxes and cannot be warded off; but the enemy's troops can be fought against, or got rid of by withdrawal or by a peace treaty,' say the teachers.
08.04.14 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.15 | Oppression by one's own troops can be warded off by winning over or removing the chiefs among the men of the constituents, or else it oppresses only one part; but the enemy's troops, oppressing the whole country, oppress by plunder, slaughter, burning, destruction and carrying away.
08.04.16 | 'Of a dispute among the constituents and a dispute with the king, a dispute among the constituents divides the constituents and brings on attacks by enemies, whereas a dispute with the king brings the constituents double rations, wages and exemptions,' say the teachers.
08.04.17 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.18 | A dispute among the constituents can be warded off by winning over the chiefs of the constituents or by removing the ground of the quarrel.
08.04.19 | Moreover, constituents who are disputing render service through their mutual rivalry.
08.04.20 | But a dispute with the king leads to the oppression and extirpation of the constituents and is to be settled by double exertion.
08.04.21 | 'Of amusement by the country and amusement by the king, amusement by the country, taking place at all three seasons, causes destruction of the fruits of works; whereas amusement by the king benefits artisans, craftsmen, performers, those who live by speech, courtesans and traders,' say the teachers.
08.04.22 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.23 | Amusement by the country consumes a little, in order to lay aside the toil of works, and having consumed it, turns again to application to works; whereas amusement by the king oppresses, both by himself and through his favourites, with forcible seizures, demands for presents, and appropriation of goods, houses and labour.
08.04.24 | 'Of the favourite queen and the prince, the prince oppresses, both by himself and through his favourites, with forcible seizures, demands for presents, and appropriation of goods, houses and labour; the favourite queen, by her enjoyment of luxuries,' say the teachers.
08.04.25 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.26 | The prince can be restrained by the minister and the chaplain; not the favourite queen, because of foolishness and association with worthless people.
08.04.27 | 'Of a guild and a chief, a guild, being uncontrollable because of its numbers, oppresses by theft and violence; a chief, by favouring and obstructing work,' say the teachers.
08.04.28 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.29 | A guild is easily turned round, because its members have the same character and the same vices, or by winning over one section of the guild's chiefs.
08.04.30 | A chief who is possessed of obstinacy oppresses by injuring the lives and property of others.
08.04.31 | 'Of the Treasurer and the Collector, the Treasurer oppresses by spoiling what has been done and by penalties; the Collector, being supervised by the office staff, enjoys the fruit assigned to him,' say the teachers.
08.04.32 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.33 | The Treasurer receives what is to be put into the treasury after it has been settled by others; the Collector, however, first makes wealth for himself and afterwards makes the king's wealth or destroys it, and in taking the property of others he acts on his own authority.
08.04.34 | 'Of the frontier officer and the traders, the frontier officer harms the trade route by letting thieves loose and by taking more than the dues; the traders, on the other hand, make it prosper by favours of goods and goods in exchange,' say the teachers.
08.04.35 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.36 | The frontier officer subsists by favouring the inflow of goods; the traders, however, combining together and raising and lowering the prices of goods, make their living at a hundred paṇas on a paṇa and a hundred kumbhas on a kumbha.
08.04.37 | Land occupied by the well-born, or land occupied by cattle herds? 'Land occupied by the well-born, even though yielding great produce, is not fit to be freed, since it serves as a source of soldiers, for fear of harm in a calamity; land occupied by cattle herds, on the other hand, being fit for cultivation, is fit to be freed,
08.04.38 | for pasture is outweighed by the cultivated field,' say the teachers.
08.04.39 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.40 | Land occupied by the well-born, even though of exceedingly great benefit, is fit to be freed, for fear of harm in a calamity; land occupied by cattle herds, on the other hand, being useful for the treasury and for draught animals, is not fit to be freed, except where it obstructs the sowing of crops.
08.04.41 | 'Of robbers and forest chiefs, robbers move in ambush by night, attack persons, are constant, carry off hundreds and thousands, provoke the principal men, and remain concealed; forest chiefs, on the other hand, move in the frontier forests, go about openly and in plain sight, and harm only one region,' say the teachers.
08.04.42 | 'No,' says Kautilya.
08.04.43 | Robbers rob the careless; they are few, dull, and easy to find out and to catch; forest chiefs, on the other hand, are settled in their own territory, numerous and valiant, fighting openly, plunderers and slayers of countries, and of the same nature as kings.
08.04.44 | Of a deer forest and an elephant forest: deer are numerous, supply much meat and hide, cause little trouble over fodder, and are easy to control;
08.04.45 | Elephants are the opposite, and while being captured, or when vicious, they lead to the ruin of the country.
08.04.46 | Of benefit from one's own sthānīya (provincial town) and from that of the enemy, benefit from one's own sthānīya provides grain, cattle, gold and raw materials and sustains the life of the country people in adversity;
08.04.47 | benefit from the enemy's sthānīya is the opposite.
08.04.48 | These are the afflictions. Internal obstruction is obstruction by chiefs; external is obstruction by an ally or forest tribes. 48. This is the group of obstructions.
08.04.49 | Afflicted by those two and by the afflictions as stated, held up with the chiefs, impaired by exemptions, scattered, wrongly collected, carried off by a neighbouring king or forest tribes: this is the group of impediments to the treasury.
08.04.50a | For the sake of the growth of the country, one should strive for the non-arising of afflictions and for warding off those that have arisen, and for the destruction of obstructions and impediments.

08.05.1 | The calamities of the army: unhonoured, dishonoured, unpaid, sick, newly arrived, come from afar, exhausted, depleted, repulsed, with its front charge destroyed, arrived in the wrong season, arrived on unsuitable ground, despairing of its hopes, run away, encumbered with women within, with a thorn within, with a disaffected base, with a split core, withdrawn, driven too far, encamped, closely joined with the enemy, besieged, surrounded, with its supplies of grain and men cut off, scattered in its own territory, scattered in an ally's territory, joined with treasonable persons, with a dangerous heel-seizer, with an empty base, assembled without a master, with its chief broken off, and blind — thus.
08.05.2 | Of these, between the unhonoured and the dishonoured, the unhonoured one would fight when it has been given wealth and honour, not the dishonoured one, which has anger within.
08.05.3 | Between the unpaid and the sick, the unpaid one would fight when its wages are paid on the spot, not the sick one, which is unfit for work.
08.05.4 | Between the newly arrived and the one come from afar, the newly arrived one would fight when it has learnt the terrain from others and is mixed with troops that are not new, not the one come from afar, which has the fatigue of a long march.
08.05.5 | Between the exhausted and the depleted, the exhausted one would fight when it has obtained rest through bathing, food and sleep, not the depleted one, whose draught animals and men have been lost in a battle elsewhere.
08.05.6 | Between the repulsed and the one whose front charge is destroyed, the repulsed one, broken in the first onset, would fight when rallied together with eminent brave men, not the one whose front charge is destroyed, whose heroes have been killed in the first onset.
08.05.7 | Between the one arrived in the wrong season and the one arrived on unsuitable ground, the one arrived in the wrong season would fight when provided with draught animals, weapons and armour suited to the season, not the one arrived on unsuitable ground, whose spreading out and movement are obstructed.
08.05.8 | Between the one despairing of its hopes and the one that has run away, the one despairing of its hopes would fight when its wish has been fulfilled, not the one that has run away, whose chief men have deserted.
08.05.9 | Between the one encumbered with women and the one with a thorn within, the one encumbered with women would fight after leaving the women behind, not the one with a thorn within, which has an enemy within.
08.05.10 | Between the one with a disaffected base and the one with a split core, the one with a disaffected base would fight when its anger has been pacified by conciliation and the other means, not the one with a split core, which is divided from one another.
08.05.11 | Between the withdrawn and the one driven too far, the withdrawn one, which has passed beyond one kingdom, would fight relying on a hiding-place and an ally, by means of counsel and activity, not the one driven too far, which has passed beyond many kingdoms, because of its many obstructions.
08.05.12 | Between the encamped and the closely joined, the encamped one, whose marching and halting are separate, would fight after outwitting the enemy, not the closely joined one, whose halting and marching are in one place with the enemy.
08.05.13 | Between the besieged and the surrounded, the besieged one would go out by another side and fight back against the besieger, not the surrounded one, which is blocked on all sides.
08.05.14 | Between the one whose grain is cut off and the one whose supply of men is cut off, the one whose grain is cut off would fight after bringing grain from elsewhere, or living on food from moving and stationary things, not the one whose supply of men is cut off, which has no reinforcement coming.
08.05.15 | Between the one scattered in its own territory and the one scattered in an ally's territory, the one scattered in its own territory, an army dispersed in its own land, can be called together in a calamity, not the one scattered in an ally's territory, because of the remoteness of place and time.
08.05.16 | Between the one joined with the treasonable and the one with a dangerous heel-seizer, the one joined with the treasonable would fight when placed under trusted men and not kept in one body, not the one with a dangerous heel-seizer, which is fearful of an attack from behind.
08.05.17 | Between the one with an empty base and the one assembled without a master, the one with an empty base would fight with its whole massed strength after arranging protection for the citizens and the country-people, not the one assembled without a master, which lacks a king and a commander of the army.
08.05.18 | Between the one with its chief broken off and the blind one, the one with its chief broken off would fight when placed under another leader, not the blind one, which has no guide. Thus.
