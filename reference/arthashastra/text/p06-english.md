06.01.1 | The king, the minister, the country, the fort, the treasury, the army and the ally are the constituent elements (prakṛtis).
06.01.2 | Among these, the excellence of the king (is as follows):
06.01.3 | Born of a high family, endowed with fortune, intelligence and spirit, heeding the elders, righteous, truthful in speech, not breaking his word, grateful, liberal and of large aims, of great energy, not dilatory, having controllable neighbouring princes, of firm mind, not having a mean council, desirous of training: these are the qualities that draw people to him (ābhigāmika qualities).
06.01.4 | Desire to learn, listening, grasping, retention, understanding, reasoning (ūha), rejection of the false (apoha) and devotion to truth are the qualities of intellect.
06.01.5 | Valour, indignation, quickness and dexterity are the qualities of energy.
06.01.6 | Eloquent, bold, possessed of memory, intellect and strength, lofty, self-controlled, trained in the arts, free from vices, a leader of the army, seeing the fitting return for benefit and for injury, possessed of modesty, employing men in adversity and in the normal state, far-seeing in time and space, giving importance to place, time, human effort and the task, able to distinguish between peace, valour, giving up, restraint, bargaining and the enemy's weak points, reserved, whose glance is not downcast, derisive, crooked or frowning, free from desire, anger, greed, obstinacy, fickleness, vexing others and slander, pleasant (śakla), speaking with a smile and with dignity, whose conduct follows the counsel of elders: this is personal excellence (ātma-sampat).
06.01.7 | The excellence of the minister has been stated before.
06.01.8 | Having strongholds both in the centre and at the frontiers, able to sustain itself and others in adversity, easily defended by itself, self-sustaining in livelihood, hostile to the enemy, having controllable neighbouring princes, free from mud, rocks, saline land, uneven ground, thorns, bands, savage beasts and forest tribes, attractive, possessing crown lands, mines, timber forests and elephant forests, suited to cattle, suited to men, with protected pastures, rich in animals, not dependent on rain, provided with water routes and land routes, with valuable, varied and abundant goods, able to bear the burden of the army and of taxes, with industrious farmers, with masters who are not foolish and (a population) mostly of the lower varṇas, with loyal and honest men: this is the excellence of the country.
06.01.9 | The excellence of the fort has been stated before.
06.01.10 | Acquired through dharma by predecessors or by himself, consisting mostly of gold and silver, with varied and large gems and bullion, (such that) it would withstand even a long calamity without income: this is the excellence of the treasury.
06.01.11 | Inherited from father and grandfather, permanent, obedient, with sons and wives content and provided for, not disappointed on expeditions abroad, unchecked everywhere, enduring hardship, having fought many battles, skilled in the science of all fighting and weapons, not of divided loyalty because it shares prosperity and decline, consisting mostly of Kṣatriyas: this is the excellence of the army.
06.01.12 | Inherited from father and grandfather, permanent, amenable, not of divided loyalty, rising (to help) in great force and quickly: this is the excellence of the ally.
06.01.13 | Not of royal seed, greedy, having a mean council, with disaffected subjects, of unjust conduct, not attentive to work, addicted to vices, without energy, relying on fate, acting at random in affairs, without a following, cowardly and always doing harm: this is the excellence of the enemy.
06.01.14 | For an enemy of such a kind is easy to exterminate.
06.01.15a | These seven constituent elements, excluding the enemy, endowed with the full rise of their own qualities, are declared, in their proper form, to be the excellences of the king, being the limbs.
06.01.15b | ~
06.01.16a | A self-possessed king brings constituent elements that lack excellence to excellence; a king who is not self-possessed destroys constituent elements that are flourishing and loyal.
06.01.16b | ~
06.01.17a | Then that king who is not self-possessed, with his constituents corrupted, even if he rules the earth to its four ends, is either killed by his constituents or passes into the power of his enemies.
06.01.17b | ~
06.01.18a | But a self-possessed king, even with a small territory, endowed with excellence of the constituents and knowing policy, conquers the whole earth indeed and does not fail.

06.02.1 | Peace and activity are the source of acquisition and security.
06.02.2 | Activity is that which secures acquisition for the undertakings of works.
06.02.3 | Peace is that which secures security for the enjoyment of the fruits of works.
06.02.4 | The sixfold policy is the source of peace and activity.
06.02.5 | Decline, stability and growth: these are its outcomes.
06.02.6 | Good policy and bad policy are the human; good fortune and misfortune are the divine.
06.02.7 | For action, divine and human, keeps the world going.
06.02.8 | The divine is what is brought about by the unseen.
06.02.9 | In that, union with a desired fruit is good fortune; with an undesired one, misfortune.
06.02.10 | The human is what is brought about by the seen.
06.02.11 | In that, the accomplishment of acquisition and security is good policy; failure is bad policy.
06.02.12 | That is to be thought about; the divine is not to be thought about.
06.02.13 | The king, endowed with the constituents of self and material means, the seat of policy, is the one wishing to conquer (vijigīṣu).
06.02.14 | Around him on all sides, forming a circle, immediately adjacent in territory, is the constituent that is the enemy.
06.02.15 | Likewise, separated by one territory, is the constituent that is the ally.
06.02.16 | A neighbouring king endowed with the excellences of an enemy is the foe; one afflicted by calamity is to be marched against; one without support or with weak support is to be exterminated; in the opposite case, he is to be harassed or weakened.
06.02.17 | These are the kinds of enemy.
06.02.18 | After him, in front, the ally, the enemy's ally, the ally's ally and the enemy's ally's ally follow in succession of territories; behind, the heel-seizer (pārṣṇigrāha, the enemy in the rear), the rear ally (ākranda), the heel-seizer's reinforcer and the rear ally's reinforcer.
06.02.19 | The one immediately adjacent in territory is the natural enemy; one of equal birth is the inborn enemy; one who is hostile or who causes hostility is the artificial enemy.
06.02.20 | The one separated by one territory is the natural ally; one related through mother or father is the inborn ally; one who has taken refuge for the sake of wealth or life is the artificial ally.
06.02.21 | The one immediately adjacent in territory to both the enemy and the conqueror, capable of favouring them whether united or disunited and of suppressing them when disunited, is the middle king (madhyama).
06.02.22 | The one outside the enemy, the conqueror and the middle king, stronger than those constituents, capable of favouring the enemy, the conqueror and the middle king whether united or disunited and of suppressing them when disunited, is the neutral king (udāsīna).
06.02.23 | These are the constituents.
06.02.24 | The conqueror, the ally and the ally's ally: his constituents are three.
06.02.25 | Those, each one joined with the five constituents of minister, country, fort, treasury and army, become a circle of eighteen.
06.02.26 | By this is explained the separate circles of the enemy, the middle king and the neutral king.
06.02.27 | Thus is the summary of the four circles.
06.02.28 | Twelve king-constituents, sixty material constituents; in sum, seventy-two.
06.02.29 | Of these, the excellences are each according to its own kind.
06.02.30 | And power and success.
06.02.31 | Power is strength.
06.02.32 | Success is happiness.
06.02.33 | Power is threefold: the strength of knowledge is the power of counsel (mantra-śakti); the strength of treasury and army is the power of lordship (prabhu-śakti); the strength of valour is the power of energy (utsāha-śakti).
06.02.34 | Likewise success too is threefold: what is attainable by the power of counsel is success of counsel; what is attainable by the power of lordship is success of lordship; what is attainable by the power of energy is success of energy.
06.02.35 | One raised up by these becomes superior; one diminished, inferior; one of equal power, equal.
06.02.36 | Therefore he should strive to instil power and success in himself, or, if he is on a level with the others, in the material constituents, in order of proximity or according to their integrity.
06.02.37 | Or he should strive to draw them away from the treasonable and the enemy.
06.02.38 | Or if he should see: 'My enemy, possessed of power, will injure his constituents by harshness of speech and punishment and by spoiling of wealth; or, possessed of success, he will fall into negligence through hunting, gambling, drink and women; he, with his constituents estranged, exhausted or negligent, will become assailable by me; or, engaged in war, he will stay with his whole force in one place or outside a fort; he, with his army massed together and separated from allies and forts, will become assailable by me; or, thinking "a strong king further off, wishing to exterminate his enemy, having exterminated him would exterminate me", he will give help to me when I am attacked by a strong king or when my undertakings have failed'; and in the desire to win over the middle king; for these and similar reasons he should wish even for the enemy's power and success.
06.02.39a | Making the kings separated by one territory the rim and the immediately adjacent ones the spokes, the leader should extend himself as the hub in the circle of constituents.

06.02.40a | For the enemy placed in the middle, between both the leader and the ally, becomes one to be exterminated or harassed, even if he is strong.
