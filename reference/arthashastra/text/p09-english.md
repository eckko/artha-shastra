09.01.1 | The conqueror, having known the strength and weakness of himself and of the enemy [in respect of] power, place, time, time of march, time of mustering troops, revolt in the rear, losses, expenses, gains and adversities, should march, being of superior strength; otherwise he should stay.
09.01.2 | “Of energy and might, energy is better
09.01.3 | For a king who is himself brave, strong, healthy, trained in weapons, even with the army as his second, is able to conquer a king possessing might.
09.01.4 | And even a small army of his becomes one that accomplishes tasks through vigour.
09.01.5 | but a king possessing might who is without energy, assailed by valour, perishes,” say the teachers.
09.01.6 | “No,” says Kautilya.
09.01.7 | A king possessing might outwits a king possessing energy by might, [by] having brought another king superior to him, [and by] having hired and bought excellent heroic men.
09.01.8 | And his army, endowed with abundant might, horses, elephants, chariots and equipment, moves everywhere unobstructed.
09.01.9 | The mighty — women, children, the lame and the blind — having conquered and bought those possessing energy, conquered the earth.
09.01.10 | “Of might and counsel, might is better
09.01.11 | For one endowed with the power of counsel becomes one of barren intellect [and] without might.
09.01.12 | and the one without might destroys his decided action of counsel, as drought [destroys] grain in the womb,” say the teachers.
09.01.13 | “No,” says Kautilya.
09.01.14 | The power of counsel is better.
09.01.15 | For a king who has wisdom and the treatise as eyes is able, even with little effort, to set up counsel, and to outwit enemies possessing energy and might by conciliation and the rest and by contrivance and secret teaching.
09.01.16 | Thus, of the powers of energy, might and counsel, the one greater in each successive [power] outwits [the other].
09.01.17 | The place is the earth.
09.01.18 | In it, the area between the Himalaya and the sea, northward, measuring a thousand yojanas across, is the field of the wheel-turner (cakravartin).
09.01.19 | There: forest, village, mountain, watery, earthy, level, uneven — [these] are the particular kinds.
09.01.20 | Among them he should employ action such as brings about the increase of his own strength.
09.01.21 | Where there is ground for the exertions of one's own army and unsuitable ground for the enemy, that is the best place; the opposite, the worst; the common [to both], the middling.
09.01.22 | Time has cold, heat and rain as its nature.
09.01.23 | Its particular kinds are: night, day, fortnight, month, season, ayana (half-year), year, yuga.
09.01.24 | Among them he should employ action such as brings about the increase of his own strength.
09.01.25 | Where there is season for the exertions of one's own army and unsuitable season for the enemy, that is the best time; the opposite, the worst; the common [to both], the middling.
09.01.26 | “Of power, place and time, however, power is better,” say the teachers.
09.01.27 | For one possessing power is able to remedy a place with low-lying and dry ground and a time with cold, heat and rain.
09.01.28 | “Place is better,” say some.
09.01.29 | “For a dog gone to dry ground drags away a crocodile, [and] a crocodile gone to low ground [drags away] a dog.”
09.01.30 | “Time is better,” say some.
09.01.31 | “By day the crow kills the owl, at night the owl [kills] the crow.”
09.01.32 | “No,” says Kautilya.
09.01.33 | For power, place and time are mutually accomplishing.
09.01.34 | Augmented by them, having arranged protection with a third or a fourth part of the army at the base, in the rear, and in the frontiers and forests, and having taken the treasury and army that are able to accomplish the task, [against an enemy] whose old food is exhausted, whose new food is not taken, whose difficult passages and allies are unprepared, he should march the Mārgaśīrṣa march to destroy his rainy-season crop and the winter fist.
09.01.35 | He should march the Caitra march to destroy his winter crop and the spring fist.
09.01.36 | [Against an enemy] whose grass, wood and water are exhausted, whose difficult passages and allies are unprepared, he should march the Jyeṣṭhāmūlīya march to destroy his spring crop and the rainy-season fist.
09.01.37 | He should march in winter to a place that is very hot or has little fodder, fuel and water.
09.01.38 | He should march in summer to a place with frost and bad weather, mostly deep and low-lying, or with dense grass and trees.
09.01.39 | When it rains he should march [to a place] suitable for the exertions of his own army and unsuitable for the enemy's.
09.01.40 | He should make a march of long duration between the Mārgaśīrṣī and the Taiṣī [marches]; of middling duration between the Caitrī and the Vaiśākhī; of short duration between the Jyeṣṭhāmūlīya and the Āṣāḍhī; [and] the fourth, being about to stay, in a calamity.
09.01.41 | The march against a calamity has been explained in the march after making war.
09.01.42 | And the teachers mostly teach: “One should march in the enemy's calamity.”
09.01.43 | “One should march at the rise of [one's] power, because of the uncertainty of calamities,” says Kautilya.
09.01.44 | Or when, having set out, he could weaken or cut off the enemy, then he should march.
09.01.45 | In a time worn out by excessive heat he should march with a force mostly of elephants.
09.01.46 | For elephants, having sweat within, become afflicted with kuṣṭha (a skin disease).
09.01.47 | Not bathing, not drinking water, and from the inner avakṣāra, they become blind.
09.01.48 | Therefore he should march with a force mostly of elephants in a place abounding in water, and when it rains.
09.01.49 | In the opposite case [he should march] with a force mostly of donkeys, camels and horses to a place with little rain and mud.
09.01.50 | When it rains he should march with the fourfold army [to a place] mostly desert.
09.01.51 | Or he should divide the march of the road according to [whether it is] level, uneven, low, dry, short or long.
09.01.52a | Or all [marches] that are to be made should be of short duration, because of the lightness of the task; or [they should be] long, because of the weightiness of the task; and the stay in the rains [should be] elsewhere.

09.02.1 | The occasions for mustering hereditary, hired, guild, ally, enemy and forest-tribe troops.
09.02.2 | The hereditary troops are in excess of what is needed to guard the base; or the hereditary troops, provided with atyāvāpa [surplus addition], might rebel at the base; or the opponent has hereditary troops that are much attached, or has strong troops, [so that] one must fight with exertion; or the road or the time is long, because the hereditary troops can bear loss and expense; or when the encounter is with much attached [troops]; or when there is distrust of the other troops, the hired and the rest, from fear of secret instigation by the one to be marched against; or on a loss of troops among all the troops — [this is] the occasion for the hereditary troops.
09.02.3 | “My hired troops are abundant and the hereditary troops few.” “The enemy's hereditary troops are few or disaffected, or his hired army is mostly weak or without strength.” “One must fight by counsel, with little exertion.” “The place or the time is short, with little loss and expense.” “My army has little āvāpa, has its secret instigation quieted, or is trusted.” “The enemy's foraging force is small [and] is to be destroyed.” — [this is] the occasion for the hired troops.
09.02.4 | “My guild troops are abundant; it is possible to station [them] at the base and on the march.” The stay abroad is short; the opponent has mostly guild troops and wishes to fight back by counsel and exertion; the dealing is with army and troops — [this is] the occasion for the guild troops.
09.02.5 | “My ally troops are abundant; it is possible to station [them] at the base and on the march.” “The stay abroad is short, and battle of exertion [is] greater than war of counsel.” “Or, having first made the ally troops fight the forest tribes, the town-station or the reinforcer, I shall afterwards fight with my own troops.” “Or my task is common with the ally.” “Or my success in the task depends on the ally.” “Or my ally is near or is one to be favoured.” “Or I shall wear down his atyāvāpa [surplus addition].” — [this is] the occasion for the ally troops.
09.02.6 | “My enemy troops are abundant; with enemy troops I shall make the town-station or the forest tribes fight; there, as for a caṇḍāla in a quarrel between a dog and a boar, there will be success of one or the other for me.” “I shall do this crushing of the thorns of the reinforcers or of the forest tribes.” — Or he should lodge the enemy's troops, excessively grown and always near, from fear of anger, except where there is suspicion of internal revolt — and the time of war with the enemy and war with the inferior — [this is] the occasion for the enemy troops.
09.02.7 | By this the occasion for the forest-tribe troops is explained.
09.02.8 | [They are] a guide on the road, suited to the enemy's ground, contrary to the enemy's way of fighting; or the enemy has mostly forest-tribe troops: “Let a bilva be struck by a bilva.” The small foraging force is to be destroyed — [this is] the occasion for the forest-tribe troops.
09.02.9 | An army that is many, stationed in many places, stated or unstated, and rises for the sake of plunder, that is the autsāhika: without rations and wages, causing plunder, forced labour and burning, divisible by the others; the indivisible [kind] is mostly alike in region, caste and craft, united, large. 9. [These are] the occasions for taking up troops.
09.02.10 | Of these, he should make the enemy and forest-tribe troops hired with raw materials or hired with plunder.
09.02.11 | Or, when the occasion for the enemy's troops has arisen, he should restrain the enemy troops, or send them elsewhere, or make them fruitless, or lodge them scattered, or dismiss them when the time has passed.
09.02.12 | He should frustrate this mustering of troops of the enemy, and bring [it] about for himself.
09.02.13 | And of these, each earlier one is better to mobilise.
09.02.14 | Because of partaking of that state, and because of constant honour and attendance, the hereditary troops are better than the hired troops.
09.02.15 | The hired troops, constantly adjacent, quick to rise, or controllable, are better than the guild troops.
09.02.16 | The guild troops, being of the country, come to one purpose, and having alike rivalry, indignation, success and gain, are better than the ally troops.
09.02.17 | The ally troops, of unlimited place and time, and because of coming to one purpose, are better than the enemy troops.
09.02.18 | Enemy troops presided over by a noble man (ārya) are better than forest-tribe troops.
09.02.19 | Those two are for the sake of plunder.
09.02.20 | When there is no plunder, and in calamity, there would be fear of a snake from those two.
09.02.21 | “Of the troops of Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas and Shudras, because of the predominance of energy, each earlier one is better to mobilise,” say the teachers.
09.02.22 | “No,” says Kautilya.
09.02.23 | The enemy might bring over the Brahmin troops by prostration.
09.02.24 | But Kshatriya troops trained in the science of weapons are better, or Vaishya and Shudra troops of abundant strength.
09.02.25 | Therefore: “The enemy has troops of this kind; this is the counterforce to him” — he should make the mustering of troops.
09.02.26 | [A force] provided with hasti-yantra, carts, garbha, lances, javelins, hāṭaka, bamboo and darts is the counterforce to the elephant troops.
09.02.27 | That very [force], consisting mostly of stones, clubs, coverings, goads and hair-seizers, is the counterforce to the chariot troops.
09.02.28 | That very [force] is the counterforce to horses; or armoured elephants, or armoured horses.
09.02.29 | Armoured chariots and foot-soldiers with coverings are the counterforce to the fourfold army.
09.02.30a | Thus the mustering of troops, the warding off of the enemy's army: he should carry it out with [his] resources, for his own troops, by options of the arms [of the army].

09.03.1 | “A small revolt later, a great gain earlier” — [in this case] the small revolt later is the more serious.
09.03.2 | For one who has marched out, the treasonable, enemies and forest chiefs foster the small later revolt from all sides — or [there is] an uprising of the constituents.
09.03.3 | In such a state, even a great earlier gain that has been obtained is swallowed up by servants, allies, losses and expenditure.
09.03.4 | Therefore he should not march [on the ground that] “the failure of the earlier gain is one in a thousand” or “the later revolt is one in a hundred”.
09.03.5 | “For misfortunes are needle-mouthed” — such is the popular saying.
09.03.6 | In the case of a later revolt he should employ conciliation, gifts, dissension and force.
09.03.7 | In the case of the earlier gain he should make the commander of the army or a prince the one who goes with the army.
09.03.8 | Or a strong king, capable of restraining a later revolt, should march to take the earlier gain.
09.03.9 | On suspicion of internal revolt he should march taking the suspected [men] with him; or, on suspicion of external revolt, [taking] the sons and wife of these.
09.03.10 | Having made restraint of the internal [revolt] and having installed a regent with many troop-groups and many chiefs, he should march — or he should not march.
09.03.11 | “Internal revolt is worse than external revolt” — this has been said earlier.
09.03.12 | The revolt of any one of the counsellor, the priest, the commander of the army and the heir-apparent is internal revolt.
09.03.13 | He should win him over by abandoning his own fault, or by the sway of another’s power and offence.
09.03.14 | Even for a great offence by the priest, confinement or avasrāvaṇa is the accomplishment; in the case of the heir-apparent, confinement or restraint, if there is another qualified son.
09.03.15 | A son, a brother or another kinsman who is king-seizing he should win over by energy; in the absence of energy, by the actions of following the seized one and of treaty, from fear of union with the enemy.
09.03.16 | He should make him trust, by gifts of land, from others or from those of that kind.
09.03.17 | He should send an army superior to him [and] self-seizing, or the neighbouring chiefs and forest chiefs; [him] set at odds by them he should outwit.
09.03.18 | He should resort to the contrivance of taking a prince in disfavour, or to the contrivance relating to another village.
09.03.19 | By this the counsellor and the commander of the army are explained.
09.03.20 | The revolt of any one of the inner ministers, excluding the counsellor and the rest, is the revolt of the inner ministers.
09.03.21 | Even there he should employ the means as is fitting.
09.03.22 | The revolt of any one of the chief of the country, the frontier officer, the forest chief and the one who has submitted with his army is external revolt.
09.03.23 | He should have him restrained by one another.
09.03.24 | Or he should have [him], propped up by a very strong fort, restrained by any one of the neighbouring chiefs, forest chiefs, members of his family and princes in disfavour.
09.03.25 | Or he should have him supported by an ally, so that he does not go to the enemy.
09.03.26 | Or a secret agent should cause him to split from the enemy — “This one, thinking you a secret agent, will make [you] attack the master alone; having gained his object he will engage the one who goes with the army among enemies and forest chiefs, in hardship or toil, or will make [him], bereft of sons and wife, dwell at the frontier,
09.03.27 | will make you, whose valour is repulsed, an article of trade with the master, or, having made a treaty with you, will propitiate the master alone,
09.03.28 | or go to his ally, [who is] drawn near.”
09.03.29 | The one who has accepted he should honour with desired aims.
09.03.30 | The refuge of the one who has not accepted he should cause to split: “That man is a secret agent planted on you.”
09.03.31 | And the secret agent should have him killed by abandoned orders, or by secret agents.
09.03.32 | Or he should summon his fellow-marchers [and] valiant men by acting according to [their] wish.
09.03.33 | The secret agent should speak to those sent by him.
09.03.34 | Such is success.
09.03.35 | He should raise these revolts of the enemy, and quell [those] of his own.
09.03.36 | Who is able to create or to quell revolt, there secret instigation is to be done.
09.03.37 | Who is true to his pledge and able to favour in action and in the attainment of the result, and to protect in disaster, there counter-instigation is to be done; and it is to be judged [whether he is] of good intent or deceitful.
09.03.38 | For a deceitful external [man] instigates the internal [man] thus — “If, having killed his master, he hands [it] over to me, I shall have a twofold gain: the killing of an enemy and the gain of land; or else ‘the enemy will kill him’ — his party of kinsmen having been killed and alarmed by the punishment for a like fault, a greater party of the unseducible will be for me; or he will be suspected even in another of that kind; and I shall have one chief of his after another killed by abandoned orders.”
09.03.39 | Or the deceitful internal [man] instigates the external [man] thus — “I shall carry off his treasury, or destroy his army, or have the corrupt master killed by this [man]; the external [man] who has accepted I shall make attack among enemies and forest chiefs — ‘let his force be attached, let enmity with him be fastened; then he will be under my control; then I shall propitiate the master alone, or take the kingdom myself’; or, having bound [him], I shall obtain both lands, that of the external [man] and that of the master; or, having summoned the hostile [one], I shall have the trusting external [man] killed; or I shall carry off his empty base.”
09.03.40 | But the one of good intent instigates for the sake of living together.
09.03.41 | With the one of good intent he should make peace; the deceitful one, having accepted “So be it”, he should outwit.
09.03.42a | Having understood thus — enemies from enemies, one’s own from one’s own, one’s own from enemies, enemies from one’s own — the self is always to be guarded by the wise from one’s own and from enemies.

09.04.1 | The depletion of draught animals and men is loss (kṣaya).
09.04.2 | The depletion of gold and grain is expenditure (vyaya).
09.04.3 | He should march when the gain is superior to those two by many advantages.
09.04.4 | Acceptable (ādeya), retakable (pratyādeya), pacifying (prasādaka), provoking (prakopaka), of short duration, of slight loss, of little expenditure, great, of rising increase, sound, righteous and leading: this is the excellence of gain.
09.04.5 | [A gain that is] easily obtained and kept, and not to be taken back by others, is acceptable (ādeya).
09.04.6 | In the opposite case [it is] retakable (pratyādeya).
09.04.7 | Taking it, or staying there, he meets destruction.
09.04.8 | Or if he should see: "Having taken the retakable [gain], I shall cause the arrangements for the protection of the treasury, army and stores [of his] to drain away; I shall make [his] mines, materials, elephant forests, embankments, dams and trade routes with their valuables taken out; I shall weaken his constituent elements, I shall draw them away, or I shall win them over by āyoga; the other [party] will provoke them by pratiyoga; or I shall make him an article of trade in his rival side; or I shall present to him a shut-out (aparuddha) ally; staying here, I shall remedy the affliction of an ally's or of my own region from thieves and from enemies; I shall make his ally or his support grasp [his] defectiveness; then [he] will accept [one who is] estranged from the enemy [and] of his family; or, having honoured [him], I shall give him land — an ally united and risen together [with me] will be mine for a long time," he should take even a retakable gain.
09.04.9 | Thus the acceptable (ādeya) and the retakable (pratyādeya) have been explained.
09.04.10 | The gain of a righteous [person], when obtained from an unrighteous [one], becomes pacifying (prasādaka) to [his] own and to others.
09.04.11 | The opposite is provoking (prakopaka).
09.04.12 | A gain that is not being obtained, [following] the advice of the ministers, becomes provoking: "This one has been made by us to take on loss and expenditure."
09.04.13 | A gain that is being obtained through disregard of the treasonable ministers becomes provoking: "This one, his purpose accomplished, will destroy us."
09.04.14 | The opposite is pacifying (prasādaka).
09.04.15 | Thus the pacifying (prasādaka) and the provoking (kopaka) have been explained.
09.04.16 | [It is] of short duration, because it is accomplishable by mere going.
09.04.17 | [It is] of slight loss, because it is accomplishable by counsel (mantra).
09.04.18 | [It is] of little expenditure, because the expenditure is only on food.
09.04.19 | [It is] great, because of immediate abundance.
09.04.20 | [It is] of rising increase, because wealth follows from it.
09.04.21 | [It is] sound, because it is free from hindrance.
09.04.22 | [It is] righteous, because of praiseworthy acquisition.
09.04.23 | [It is] leading, because it goes [forward] without the insistence of the associates (sāmavāyika).
09.04.24 | When gains are equal, having weighed place and time, power and means, the pleasing and the unpleasing, speed and slowness, nearness and distance, the immediate and the sequel, solidity and continuity, and abundance and multiplicity of advantages, he should take the gain endowed with many advantages.

09.05.6 | When those have been quieted, the instigators would not be able to instigate others.
09.05.7 | For the internal ones are hard for the external ones to instigate, or [so are] the others for them.

09.06.1 | From the treasonable and from enemies, the pure [one] is twofold.
09.06.2 | When [it] is pure of the treasonable, [he] should apply the means that exclude force toward the townsmen or the country people.
09.06.3 | For force is impossible to cast upon a great multitude.

09.07.1 | Arrogance, of which desire is the first, provokes one's own constituent elements; bad policy [provokes] the external ones.
09.07.2 | Both of these are a demonic course of conduct.
09.07.3 | Revolt (kopa) is the alteration of one's own people.
09.07.4 | In the causes of another's growth [there are] a gain that is an adversity, a loss, and a doubt.
09.07.5 | The gain which, [when] not obtained, brings about the enemy's growth; [when] obtained, becomes [something] to be taken back by others; or, while being obtained, becomes one with loss and expenditure as its outcome - that becomes a gain that is an adversity.
09.07.6 | As: an acquisition that has become bait for the neighbours and is born of a neighbour's calamity; an acquisition sought by the enemy or obtainable through [its] own nature; an acquisition in front, opposed from behind by revolt or by the heel-seizer; an acquisition opposed by the circle through the destruction of allies or a breach of treaty - such is the gain that is an adversity.
09.07.7 | The arising of fear from oneself or from another - such is a loss.
09.07.8 | Of these two, [there is] doubt: “a gain, or not?”; “a loss, or not?”; “[is the] gain a loss?”; “[is the] loss a gain?”
09.07.9 | Doubt: “To rouse the enemy's ally - [is it] a gain, or not?”
09.07.10 | Doubt: “To draw in the enemy's army by wealth and honour - [is it] a loss, or not?”
09.07.11 | Doubt: “To take land having strong neighbours - [is the] gain a loss?”
09.07.12 | Doubt: “A joint march with a stronger one - [is the] loss a gain?”
09.07.13 | Of these, he should approach [the case of] doubt about gain.
09.07.14 | A gain with gain as its sequel; a gain without sequel; a gain with loss as its sequel; a loss with gain as its sequel; a loss without sequel; a loss with loss as its sequel - such is the sixfold group of sequels.
09.07.15 | Having uprooted an enemy, the taking by the heel-seizer - a gain with loss as its sequel.
09.07.16 | Favouring the neutral king with the army, by the result - a gain without sequel.
09.07.17 | The uprooting from within of another - a gain with loss as its sequel.
09.07.18 | Favour to the enemy's neighbour with treasury and army - a loss with loss as its sequel.
09.07.19 | Having roused one of weak power, withdrawal - a loss without sequel.
09.07.20 | Having stirred up a stronger one, withdrawal - a loss with loss as its sequel.
09.07.21 | Of these, each earlier one is better to encounter.
09.07.22 | Such is the setting in order of the undertakings.
09.07.23 | The simultaneous arising of gain from all sides is, from all sides, an adversity of gain.
09.07.24 | That same [adversity], opposed by the heel-seizer, becomes from all sides an adversity of doubt about gain.
09.07.25 | In those two, success [comes] from the support of the ally and the rear ally.
09.07.26 | From all sides, the arising of fear from enemies is, from all sides, an adversity of loss.
09.07.27 | That same [adversity], opposed by an ally, becomes from all sides an adversity of doubt about loss.
09.07.28 | In those two, success [comes] from the support of the unsteady enemy and the rear ally, or non-remedy of [what is] mixed with the other.
09.07.29 | “Acquisition from this side, acquisition from the other side”: from both sides there is an adversity of gain.
09.07.30 | And in that [adversity] with gain on all sides, he should march to take the gain endowed with the quality of acquisition.
09.07.31 | When the quality of acquisition is equal, he should march to take that - the chief, the near, the not slipping away [gain], or [the one] by which he would be deficient.
09.07.32 | “Loss from this side, loss from the other side”: from both sides there is an adversity of loss.
09.07.33 | And in that [adversity] with loss on all sides, he should seek to obtain success from allies.
09.07.34 | In the absence of an ally, he should overcome [the adversity] with loss on one side by the lesser of the constituent elements, [that] with loss on both sides by the greater, [and that] with loss on all sides he should remedy by the root.
09.07.35 | When [this is] impossible, having abandoned everything, he should go away.
09.07.36 | For the coming back again of one who lives is seen, as with Suyatra and Udayana.
09.07.37 | “Acquisition from this side, assault on the kingdom from the other side”: from both sides there is an adversity of gain and loss.
09.07.38 | In that [adversity], he should march to take the gain which overcomes the loss.
09.07.39 | Otherwise, indeed, he should ward off the assault on the kingdom.
09.07.40 | By this, the adversity of gain and loss from all sides is explained.
09.07.41 | “Loss from this side, doubt about gain from the other side”: [the adversity is] one of loss and of doubt about gain from both sides.
09.07.42 | In that [adversity], he should first overcome the loss; on that being accomplished, [he should overcome] the doubt about gain.
09.07.43 | By this, [the adversity of] doubt of loss and gain on all sides is explained.
09.07.44 | “Gain on this side, doubt of loss on the other” — the adversity of doubt of gain and loss on both sides.
09.07.45 | By this, [the adversity of] doubt of gain and loss on all sides is explained.
09.07.46 | In that, one should strive to free each earlier one of the constituent elements from doubts of loss.
09.07.47 | For better is the ally standing in doubt of loss, not the army; or the army, not the treasury.
09.07.48 | In the absence of complete freeing, one should strive to free parts of the constituent elements.
