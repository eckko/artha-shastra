12.01.1 | "A weak king attacked by a stronger one should remain submissive in every way, behaving like a reed.
12.01.2 | For he who bows to the stronger one bows to Indra," so says Bharadvaja.
12.01.3 | "He should fight with the whole mass of his forces.
12.01.4 | For valour drives away calamity.
12.01.5 | And this is the kṣatriya's own dharma: victory or defeat in battle," so says Vishalaksha.
12.01.6 | "No," says Kautilya.
12.01.7 | One who is submissive in every way lives without hope for his life, like a sheep kept in the house.
12.01.8 | And one fighting with a small army perishes, like a man plunging into the ocean without a boat.
12.01.9 | But, having taken refuge with a king superior to that one, or in an unassailable fort, he should make his efforts.
12.01.10 | Attackers are of three kinds: the righteous conqueror, the greedy conqueror and the demonic conqueror.
12.01.11 | Among them, the righteous conqueror is satisfied with submission.
12.01.12 | He should submit to him, also out of fear of the others.
12.01.13 | The greedy conqueror is satisfied by seizing land and goods.
12.01.14 | He should submit to him with wealth.
12.01.15 | The demonic conqueror (is satisfied) by seizing land, goods, sons, wives and life.
12.01.16 | Having won him over with land and goods, he should take counter-measures while remaining beyond his grasp.
12.01.17 | Any one of them rising against him he should counter by a treaty, by a war of counsel or by treacherous warfare.
12.01.18 | (He should win over) the enemy party of that (attacker) by conciliation and gifts, and his own party by dissension and force.
12.01.19 | Secret agents should destroy his fort, country or camp with weapons, poison and fire.
12.01.20 | He should have his rear seized on all sides.
12.01.21 | Or he should have his kingdom ravaged by forest tribes, or have it seized by a kinsman of his family and a prince in disfavour.
12.01.22 | And when these injuries have been done, he should send an envoy to him.
12.01.23 | Or, without doing harm, (there may be) a treaty.
12.01.24 | Even so, he should sue the one marching against him for a treaty (offering) treasury and army increased by a quarter, or increased with every day and night.
12.01.25 | If he asks for a treaty of army, he should give him useless elephants and horses, or spirited ones that have been given poison.
12.01.26 | If he asks for a treaty of men, he should give him troops of treasonable men, enemies and forest tribes, placed under the command of secret agents.
12.01.27 | He should so act that the destruction of both would result.
12.01.28 | Or he should give him a force of fierce men which, when slighted, would turn hostile, or hereditary troops loyal (to himself) which would do him harm in his calamity.
12.01.29 | If he asks for a treaty of treasury, he should give him valuables for which he would find no buyer, or raw material (kupya) unfit for war.
12.01.30 | If he asks for a treaty of land, he should give him land that can be taken back, that has a permanent enemy, that is without a place of support, or whose settlement involves great loss and expense.
12.01.31 | Or he should sue the stronger one for a treaty with all his possessions, excepting the capital.
12.01.32a | What another would take by force, that he should give up by stratagem; he should protect his own body, not his wealth, for what pity is there for wealth that is impermanent?

12.02.1 | If he does not abide by the peace, (the envoy) should tell him: "These kings, under the sway of the group of six enemies, have perished; you ought not to follow the path of those who were not self-possessed.
12.02.2 | Have regard for dharma and wealth.
12.02.3 | For those who make you undertake rashness, unrighteousness and transgression of wealth are enemies wearing the faces of friends.
12.02.4 | To fight with brave men who have given up their lives is rashness; to cause loss of men on both sides is unrighteousness; to abandon an assured gain and an uncorrupted ally is transgression of wealth.
12.02.5 | And that king has allies; further, with this wealth he will set in motion allies who will march against you from all sides.
12.02.6 | And he is not abandoned by the middle and the neutral king, or by the circle; but you are abandoned, since they look on at you, fully prepared (for war), (thinking) "Let him be further subjected to loss and expense, and let him be cut off from his ally; then, his base abandoned, we shall easily exterminate him."
12.02.7 | So you ought not to listen to enemies wearing the faces of friends, to alarm your allies and to join your enemies with good fortune, or to incur peril to life and calamity" — thus he should restrain him.
12.02.8 | Even so, if he sets out (on the march), he should cause disaffection of his constituent elements, as has been explained in the Conduct of Corporations and in the Yogavamana.
12.02.9 | And (he should cause) the employment of assassins and poisoners.
12.02.10 | What has been stated in the Protection of One's Person as things to be guarded against, there he should employ assassins and poisoners.
12.02.11 | Keepers of courtesans should drive the chiefs of the army mad with women of supreme beauty and youth.
12.02.12 | When desire for one (woman) has arisen in many, or in two chiefs, the assassins should stir up quarrels.
12.02.13 | In the quarrel, they should engage the defeated side in going away elsewhere, or in giving help to the expedition of (their) master.
12.02.14 | Or agents in the guise of holy men should, by means of love-winning herbs, for the purpose of overreaching (the victims), cause those under the sway of love to administer poison among the chiefs.
12.02.15 | Or an agent in the guise of a trader, having showered with money, on the pretext of love, a maidservant close to the king's favourite chief queen, should abandon her.
12.02.16 | An agent in the guise of a holy man, pointed out (to her) by an agent in the guise of that very (trader's) servant, should give (her) a love-winning herb, (saying) "it is to be applied on the trader's body."
12.02.17 | When this has succeeded, he should teach the favourite (queen) too this same device: "it is to be applied on the king's body."
12.02.18 | Then he should overreach (the king) with poison.
12.02.19 | Or an agent in the guise of a fortune-teller should tell a high official who has been won over step by step that he is "endowed with the marks of a king."
12.02.20 | A female mendicant (should tell) his wife: "You will become a king's wife or the mother of a king."
12.02.21 | Or a woman agent in the guise of (his) wife should tell the high official: "The king, it is said, will confine me (in his harem); this letter and this ornament have been brought by a wandering nun for your presence."
12.02.22 | Or an agent in the guise of a cook or kitchen-hand should present to him a message of the king, together with tempting money, for the administering of poison.
12.02.23 | An agent in the guise of a trader should corroborate that to him, and should declare the accomplishment of the task.
12.02.24 | Thus, by one, two or three such means, he should bring each high official of his to an attack or to going away. Thus (ends the section).
12.02.25 | And in his forts, secret agents close to the regent should report among the citizens and country people, under the pretext of friendship: "The soldiers and the officials have been told by the regent: 'The king, fallen into difficulty, will return alive, or not; acquire wealth by force, and kill the enemies.'"
12.02.26 | When this has spread widely, the assassins should have the citizens robbed at night, and should strike down the chiefs, (saying) "Thus are those dealt with who do not obey the regent."
12.02.27 | And in the regent's places they should leave behind blood-stained weapons, money and fetters.
12.02.28 | Then the secret agents should report: "The regent is causing killings and plunder."
12.02.29 | In the same way they should alienate the country people from the Collector (samāhartṛ).
12.02.30 | But the assassins, having killed the Collector's men in the midst of villages at night, should say: "Thus are those dealt with who oppress the country unjustly."
12.02.31 | When the grievance has arisen, they should have the regent or the Collector killed through the anger of the constituent elements.
12.02.32 | Or they should install (in his place) a member of his family or one who is in disfavour.
12.02.33a | They should burn the harem, the city gate and the stores of goods and grain, or should kill those (officers); and, speaking as men in distress, should tell him (of it).

12.03.1 | Secret agents close to the king and to the king's favourites should tell, with the confidence of a friend, those among the chiefs of the infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants who stand in the position of friends to them: "The king is angry."
12.03.2 | When this has become widespread, the assassins, having made counter-measures against the night patrol, should say in their houses: "By the master's word, come."
12.03.3 | They should strike them down just as they are coming out, and should say to those nearby: "It is the master's message."
12.03.4 | And to those who have not been done away with, the secret agents should say: "This is what we told you; one who wishes to live must get away."
12.03.5 | And to those to whom the king, when asked, does not give, the secret agents should say: "The regent has been told by the king: 'So-and-so and so-and-so ask me for a thing not fit to be asked; I have refused them; they are in league with the enemy; strive to remove them.'"
12.03.6 | Then he should act as before.
12.03.7 | And to those to whom the king, when asked, gives, the secret agents should say: "The regent has been told by the king: 'So-and-so and so-and-so ask me for a thing not fit to be asked; that thing has been given to them by me to win their confidence; they are in league with the enemy; strive to remove them.'"
12.03.8 | Then he should act as before.
12.03.9 | And to those who do not ask him for a thing fit to be asked, the secret agents should say: "The regent has been told by the king: 'So-and-so and so-and-so do not ask me for a thing fit to be asked; what else can it be but from fear of their own guilt? Strive to remove them.'"
12.03.10 | Then he should act as before.
12.03.11 | By this the whole party of the seducible is explained.
12.03.12 | Or a secret agent close to the king should make the king believe: "This and this high officer of yours is conversing with the enemy's men."
12.03.13 | When the king has accepted it, he should show him treasonable men carrying his written messages, saying: "This is it."
12.03.14 | Or, having tempted the army chiefs and the men of the constituent elements with land or gold, he should make them attack their own people or draw them away.
12.03.15 | The son of his who lives nearby or in a fort, him he should have instigated through a secret agent: "You are the son better endowed in qualities, yet you are kept in the background; why then do you stay indifferent? Attack and seize it, before the heir-apparent destroys you."
12.03.16 | Or, having tempted with gold a member of his family or a prince in disfavour, he should say: "Crush his inner army, his frontier contingent, or his border region."
12.03.17 | Having won over the forest chieftains with wealth and honour, he should have his kingdom ravaged.
12.03.18 | Or he should say to his heel-seizer: "This king, indeed, having destroyed me, will destroy you; seize his heel; when he has turned back against you, I shall seize his heel."
12.03.19 | Or he should say to his allies: "I am your dam; when I am broken, this king will sweep you all away; or, joining together, let us wreck his expedition."
12.03.20 | He should send word to those leagued with him and to those not leagued: "This king, indeed, having uprooted me, will act against you; be aware; it is better for you to come to my aid."
12.03.21a | Or again he should send word to the middle king or to the neutral king, whichever is nearer, for the sake of his own release, surrendering to him with all his possessions.

12.04.1 | And those agents of his (the weaker king) who are in the forts disguised as traders, in the villages disguised as householders, and at the frontiers of the country disguised as cowherds and ascetics, should send word to the neighbouring princes, the forest chieftains, the kinsmen of that (enemy) and princes in disfavour, preceded by (gifts of) goods and dwellings: "This country can be seized."
12.04.2 | And having honoured with wealth and honour the secret agents of these (neighbours etc.) who have come into the fort, they should show them the weak points of the (enemy's) constituent elements.
12.04.3 | They should strike at those (weak points) together with them.
12.04.4 | Or in his (the enemy's) camp an agent disguised as a liquor-seller, having set up a condemned man as his son and having done away with him with poison at the time of an assault, should give out hundreds of jars of liquor mixed with madana-juice, saying "this is the funeral libation."
12.04.5 | Or he should give pure liquor, or liquor mixed by a quarter, for one day, and afterwards give out (liquor) prepared with poison.
12.04.6 | Or having given pure liquor to the chiefs of the army, he should give out (liquor) prepared with poison at the time of their intoxication.
12.04.7 | Or (for an agent) disguised as an army chief, the procedure is the same as stated: "a condemned man (set up) as his son".
12.04.8 | Or agents disguised as sellers of cooked meat, of cooked rice, of auṇḍika-food and of cakes, having proclaimed special goods and, through mutual rivalry, saying "on credit" or "cheaper", having called in the enemies, should spoil their own goods with poison.
12.04.9 | Or women and children, having received liquor, milk, curds, ghee and oils in the hands of (i.e. from) the dealers in them, should pour them into their own vessels containing poison.
12.04.10 | Saying "at this price, or let something better be given again," they should pour it back right there (into the dealer's stock).
12.04.11 | These same (acts should be done by) agents disguised as traders, or by those who bring (goods) by way of selling goods.
12.04.12 | Those stationed near (the enemy) should put poison into the feed and fodder of elephants and horses.
12.04.13 | Or agents disguised as labourers should sell fodder or water smeared with poison.
12.04.14 | Or cattle-traders long associated (with the enemy's people) should, at the times of an assault, let loose herds of cattle or of goats and sheep in the enemies' places of confusion, and also vicious ones among horses, donkeys, camels, buffaloes and the like.
12.04.15 | Or (agents) disguised as them should (let loose animals) whose eyes are smeared with the blood of a musk-rat.
12.04.16 | Or agents disguised as hunters should release wild beasts from cages, or snake-catchers (release) snakes of fierce venom, or those who live by elephants (release) elephants.
12.04.17 | Or those who live by fire should let loose fire.
12.04.18 | Or secret agents should strike down the chiefs of the infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants when they are turned away (off guard), or should set fire to the quarters of the chiefs.
12.04.19 | Or agents planted (beforehand) in the guise of the treasonable, of enemies and of forest chieftains should make an attack in the rear or receive (meet) an assault.
12.04.20 | Or (agents) hidden in the forest, having drawn out the frontier contingent, should strike it down, or (strike) on a single path (a narrow road) the supply trains, the reinforcements and the foraging parties.
12.04.21 | Or in a night battle, by a pre-arranged signal, having sounded many war-instruments, they should say: "We have entered; the kingdom is taken."
12.04.22 | Or having entered the king's quarters, they should kill the king in the tumults.
12.04.23 | Or when he has marched out, troops of mlecchas, forest tribes and the army, relying on an ambush or relying on an enclosure of posts, should kill him from all sides.
12.04.24 | Or agents disguised as hunters should strike (him) in the tumults of an assault with the means of secret warfare.
12.04.25 | Or on a single path, or in a place with rock, an enclosure of posts, mire, or water in between, they should strike with a force on its own ground.
12.04.26 | Or they should flood (him) with the rush of water from breaching the embankment of a river, a lake or a tank.
12.04.27 | Or (him) stationed in a desert fort, a forest fort or a low-lying fort, they should destroy with fire and smoke produced by secret contrivance (yoga).
12.04.28 | Assassins (tīkṣṇa) should finish him off with fire when he has gone into a narrow place, with smoke when he has gone into a desert, with poison when he has gone into a store-room, with vicious crocodiles or with men moving in the water when he has plunged into water, or (kill him) as he rushes out from his burning quarters.
12.04.29a | By contrivance and by the vāmana-contrivance, or by any other contrivance, he should outwit the enemy caught in the grounds stated above.

12.05.1 | In a procession for the worship of a deity the enemy has, out of devotion, many places of worship and of arrival.
12.05.2 | There he should set up a contrivance against him.
12.05.3 | When he has entered the temple, he should, by releasing a machine, make a hidden wall or a stone fall on him from above.
12.05.4 | He should let loose from the topmost chamber a shower of stones and weapons, or a door-panel made to fall down, or a bar set in the wall or fastened at one end.
12.05.5 | Or he should make the deity's body, banner or weapons fall on him from above.
12.05.6 | Or on the grounds where he stands, sits and walks he should spread poison by a plaster of cow-dung, by a sprinkling of scented water, or by an offering of flowers and powder.
12.05.7 | Or he should convey to him a pungent smoke concealed by perfume.
12.05.8 | Or, with a pit of stakes or a pitfall below the bed or seat, its floor held by a machine, he should make him fall in by releasing a pin.
12.05.9 | Or when the enemy is near, he should bring over from the country the people able to bear a siege.
12.05.10 | And he should remove from the fort those unable to bear a siege, or send them to an enemy territory that is to be taken back.
12.05.11 | And he should settle the country people, gathered in one place, in mountain, forest or river forts, or in places separated by a wilderness, under the charge of a son or brother.
12.05.12 | The causes of a siege have been explained in the section on the conduct of one submitting with his army.
12.05.13 | He should have grass and wood burnt for up to a yojana.
12.05.14 | And he should contaminate the waters and drain them away.
12.05.15 | And outside he should set up wells, concealed traps, pitfalls and thorny barriers.
12.05.16 | Having made a tunnel with many openings to the enemy's position, he should have the chiefs in charge of stores carried off, or the enemy himself.
12.05.17 | Or when a tunnel is employed by the enemy, he should have a moat dug reaching down to water, or a row of wells along the rampart.
12.05.18 | He should place water-jars or bell-metal vessels at suspected places for the detection of digging.
12.05.19 | When the course of the tunnel is known, he should have a counter-tunnel made.
12.05.20 | Having broken through in the middle, he should let in smoke or water.
12.05.21 | Or, having provided for the defence of the fort and placed an heir at the base, he should go in the direction opposite to the enemy; or go whence he might join allies, kinsmen or forest chiefs, or the enemy's powerful enemies or treasonable men; or whence, having gone, he might separate the enemy from his allies, seize his rear, make him lose his kingdom, or stop his supplies, reinforcements and foraging parties; or whence he might be able, like a dice-player, to strike at him with a throw; or whence he might protect his own kingdom or bring about an increase of his base.
12.05.22 | Or he should go to that place from which he might obtain the peace he desires.
12.05.23 | Or those who set out with him should send word: "This enemy of yours has come into our hands; on the pretext of merchandise or of an injury, send gold and a force with a strong core, to which we may hand him over, bound or put to death."
12.05.24 | When this is agreed, he should take the gold and the picked force.
12.05.25 | Or the frontier officer, on the pretext of handing over a fort, having led a part of the enemy's army inside, should have it killed when it has become trusting.
12.05.26 | Or he should invite the enemy's army to kill the country people gathered in one place.
12.05.27 | Having led it into an enclosed region, he should have it killed when it has become trusting.
12.05.28 | Or one in the guise of a friend should send word to the one outside: "Grain, oils, sugar or salt are exhausted in this fort; it will come in at such a place and time; seize it."
12.05.29 | Then treasonable men, enemies and forest chiefs, or others given up to death, should bring in grain, oil, sugar or salt mixed with poison.
12.05.30 | By this the seizing of all goods and supplies is explained.
12.05.31 | Or, having made peace, he should give him a part of the gold, delaying over the rest.
12.05.32 | Then he should get the protective arrangements slackened.
12.05.33 | Or he should strike with fire, poison and weapons.
12.05.34 | Or he should win over with favours those favourites of his who accept gold.
12.05.35 | Or, when completely exhausted, he should give him the fort and go out.
12.05.36 | He should go out by a tunnel, by a breach in the flank, or by breaking the rampart.
12.05.37 | Having made an assault at night, if successful he should stay; if unsuccessful he should go away by the flank.
12.05.38 | He should go out in the disguise of a heretical ascetic, with a small retinue.
12.05.39 | Or, in the guise of a corpse, he should be carried out by secret agents.
12.05.40 | Or, wearing a woman's dress, he should follow a corpse.
12.05.41 | Or at offerings to deities, ancestral rites or sacrificial feasts, having set out food and drink mixed with poison,
12.05.42 | having made secret instigation, he should sally out with agents in the guise of treasonable men and strike with his hidden army.
12.05.43 | Or, his fort thus taken, having set up a shrine with a feast of offerings, he should enter and stay in a hollow of the deity's image, or in a hidden wall, or in an underground chamber fitted with a deity's image.
12.05.44 | When the matter has been forgotten, he should enter the royal residence at night by a tunnel and kill the enemy while asleep.
12.05.45 | Or, having loosened a detachable machine, he should make him fall down below.
12.05.46 | Or he should set fire to the enemy sleeping in a house smeared with a mixture of poison and fire, or in a house of lac.
12.05.47 | Or in one of the resting places among the pleasure-grove and recreation spots, assassins who have entered through an underground chamber, a tunnel or a hidden wall should kill him when off guard, or men secretly stationed should kill him with poison.
12.05.48 | Or when he is sleeping in an enclosed place, hidden women should let loose snakes, poison, fire or smoke on him from above.
12.05.49 | Or when an occasion arises, one moving in secret should employ against the enemy, when he has gone into the inner quarters, whatever may be feasible.
12.05.50 | Then he should go away quite secretly, and he should display the signal to his own people.
12.05.51a | Having summoned by trumpet signals the door-keepers, eunuch attendants and others secretly placed with the enemy, he should have the remnants of the enemy killed.
