10.01.1 | On a site approved by the site-expert, the nāyaka (commander), the carpenter and the astrologers should measure out the camp: circular, oblong or four-cornered, or according to the terrain; with four gates, six roads and nine divisions, furnished with moat, rampart, wall, gates and towers, both in a place of danger and at a halting place.
10.01.2 | To the north of the middle (division), in the ninth part, is the king's quarters, a hundred dhanus in length and half of that in width; in its western half, the inner apartments (antaḥpura).
10.01.3 | And the troop of inner guards (antarvaṃśika) should encamp at its edge.
10.01.4 | In front, the audience hall; on the right, the offices for treasury, edicts and business; on the left, the station of the king's riding elephants, horses and chariots.
10.01.5 | Beyond this, at intervals of a hundred dhanus, are four enclosures made of carts, posts (methī), creeper-hedges, pillars and walls.
10.01.6 | In the first (enclosure), in front, the minister and the chaplain; on the right, the storehouse and the kitchen; on the left, the store of raw materials and weapons.
10.01.7 | In the second, the station of the hereditary and hired troops, of the horses and chariots, and of the commander of the army.
10.01.8 | In the third, the elephants, the guild troops and the praśāstṛ (camp superintendent).
10.01.9 | In the fourth, the labour corps, the nāyaka, and the troops of allies, of enemies and of forest tribes, supervised by (the king's) own men.
10.01.10 | Traders and courtesans (rūpājīvā) along the main road.
10.01.11 | Outside, hunters with packs of dogs, equipped with trumpets and fires; and hidden guards.
10.01.12 | And on the enemies' approach route he should set up wells, concealed traps, pitfalls and thorny barriers.
10.01.13 | He should have the guard duty of the eighteen groups rotated.
10.01.14 | And he should have a watch kept by day as well, for the sake of detecting spies.
10.01.15 | And he should have quarrels, drinking, gatherings and gambling prevented, and the passes (mudrā) guarded.
10.01.16 | The śūnyapāla should arrest a soldier who has come back from the army without orders.
10.01.17a | The praśāstṛ should go ahead on the route in proper order, and should have protective works and water supplies made by the carpenters and the labour corps.

10.02.1 | Having reckoned, according to fodder, fuel and water, the halting places on the road through villages and forests, and the time for halting, staying and marching, he should set out on the expedition.
10.02.2 | He should have food and equipment carried along, twice the amount needed to make up for those (shortages).
10.02.3 | Or, if unable (to do that), he should distribute it among the troops, or should store it up at intermediate places (on the way).
10.02.4 | In front the leader, in the middle the royal household and the king, on the two flanks the horses as the flank guard, at the ends of the wheel (the outer edges) the elephants or an extension of the foragers, behind the commander of the army: so he should march and encamp.
10.02.5 | The foraging force (prasāra) is (the body) that lives off the forest on all sides.
10.02.6 | The supply line (vīvadha) is the continuous supply following from one's own territory.
10.02.7 | The reinforcer (āsāra) is the ally's force.
10.02.8 | The place of retreat (apasāra) is the station of the royal household.
10.02.9 | In case of an attack from the front he should march in the makara (crocodile) array, from the rear in the śakaṭa (cart) array, on the flanks in the vajra (thunderbolt) array, from all sides in the sarvatobhadra (secure on all sides) array, and on a single-track path in the sūcī (needle) array.
10.02.10 | When the road becomes twofold, he should go by the one that leads through ground suited to himself.
10.02.11 | For in battle, those standing on their own ground are adverse to those who are not on suitable ground.
10.02.12 | One yojana is the lowest (daily) march, one and a half the middling, two yojanas the highest; or the march is as much as is feasible.
10.02.13 | (Thinking:) 'A heel-seizer, a reinforcer, a middle or a neutral king who would give shelter (to the enemy) or destroy what has been achieved has to be countered; a narrow road has to be cleared; the treasury, the army, the forces of the ally, the enemy or the forest tribes, or the season for labourers, have to be waited for; the exhaustion of the fort-works, stores and defences (the enemy) has made, the despondency of (his) hired troops and of (his) ally's troops will come about; or the instigators (planted in the enemy's camp) are not urging haste; or the enemy will fulfil (our) intended purpose' — he should march slowly; in the opposite case, quickly.
10.02.14 | He should have (the army) cross waters by means of elephants, pillar-bridges, causeways, boats and rafts of timber and bamboo, and with gourds, skins, baskets, leather bags, floats, logs and bamboo plaits.
10.02.15 | When the ford is held (by the enemy), he should cross over elsewhere at night with elephants and horses and take up an ambush.
10.02.16 | In a waterless region he should have wheeled vehicles and four-footed animals carry water according to the length of the road, as far as he is able.
10.02.17 | When (an army) has a long wilderness to cross, is without water or lacking fodder, fuel and water, is on a hard road, scattered by an attack, wearied by hunger, thirst and the road, engaged in climbing up and coming out of rivers, ravines or mountains deep with mud or water, crowded together on a single-track path, on rugged mountain ground or in a defile, is encamping or setting out, is without armour, busy eating, exhausted from a long march, asleep, afflicted by disease, pestilence and famine, has its foot-soldiers, horses and elephants sick, is not on suitable ground, or is in (other) calamities of the army, he should protect his own army (in such states) and strike the enemy's army (in such states).
10.02.18 | Knowledge of the enemy's strength, when (his army) has marched along a single-track path, (is obtained) by counting the army's droppings, fodder, food, bedding litter, fireplaces, flags and weapons.
10.02.19 | He should conceal those (traces) of his own.
10.02.20a | Having placed at his back, on his own ground, a mountain fort or a forest fort provided with a retreat and a reserve, he should fight and encamp.

10.03.1 | One who is superior in strength, who has carried out instigation (of the enemy's men), and who has made provision against the season, should resort to open battle on his own ground.
10.03.2 | In the opposite case, (he should resort to) treacherous warfare.
10.03.3 | He should strike the enemy at times of calamity to the (enemy's) troops or of an assault; or, himself standing on his own ground, (strike) the enemy standing on unfavourable ground; or, having a hold over the (enemy's) constituent elements, (strike) the enemy standing on his own ground.
10.03.4 | Or, having given a (feigned) rout with troops of the treasonable, of enemies and of forest tribes, he should strike (the enemy) when he has reached unfavourable ground.
10.03.5 | He should break up a compact (enemy) army with elephants.
10.03.6 | Having first given a (feigned) rout, he, himself unbroken, should turn back and strike (the enemy) who has scattered in pursuit and is broken (in formation).
10.03.7 | Having struck from the front, he should strike (the enemy), wavering or turned away, from the rear with elephants and horses.
10.03.8 | Having struck from the rear, he should strike (the enemy), wavering or turned away, from the front with his best troops.
10.03.9 | By these two, the two attacks on the flanks are explained.
10.03.10 | Or, from the side where (the enemy's) treasonable or weak troops are, from that side he should strike.
10.03.11 | When the ground is uneven in front, he should strike from the rear.
10.03.12 | When the ground is uneven at the rear, he should strike from the front.
10.03.13 | When the ground is uneven on one flank, he should strike from the other side.
10.03.14 | Or, having first made (the enemy) fight with troops of the treasonable, of enemies and of forest tribes, he, himself unwearied, should strike the enemy when wearied.
10.03.15 | Or, having himself given a (feigned) rout with the treasonable troops, he, himself wary and with an ambush position as his resort, should strike (the enemy) who has become confident, thinking "it is won".
10.03.16 | He, himself not careless, should strike (the enemy) made careless by plundering caravans, herds, camps and transport convoys.
10.03.17 | Or, with his best troops screened by weak troops, having penetrated among the enemy's warriors, he should strike.
10.03.18 | Having drawn out the enemy's warriors by a seizure of cattle or by the killing of wild beasts (a hunt), he, hidden in an ambush position, should strike.
10.03.19 | Having kept (them) awake at night by an assault, he should strike by day those exhausted with drowsiness or fallen asleep.
10.03.20 | Or he should deliver an attack on the sleeping (enemy) with elephants whose leather coverings extend to their feet.
10.03.21 | He should strike in the afternoon those exhausted by being under arms all day.
10.03.22 | With dry-leather bags holding round pebbles, or with timid herds of cattle, buffaloes and camels, he, himself unbroken, should strike (the enemy) whose elephants and horses are untrained, (once he is) broken and turned back.
10.03.23 | Or he should strike the whole (enemy force) that faces the sun and the wind.
10.03.24 | Desert, forest, a narrow pass, mud, a hill, low ground, uneven ground, boats, cattle, a cart array, fog and night: these are the ambush positions.
10.03.25 | And the earlier mentioned times for striking are grounds for treacherous warfare.
10.03.26 | But a battle with place and time specified (in advance) is the most in accord with dharma.
10.03.27 | Having assembled the army, he should say: "I receive the same pay (as you); this kingdom is to be enjoyed together with you; the enemy is to be struck by you as instructed by me."
10.03.28 | In the Vedas too it is heard, at the final baths of sacrifices whose fees have been fully paid: "That is your goal, which is that of heroes."
10.03.29 | Here too there are two verses.
10.03.30a | Those (worlds) which brahmins seeking heaven reach by multitudes of sacrifices, by austerity and by heaps of (gifts to) worthy recipients, even beyond those do heroes pass in a moment, giving up their lives in good battles.

10.03.32 | Through the minister and the chaplain he should encourage the fighters by the excellence of the battle array.
10.03.33 | And his group of fortune-tellers and the rest should, by proclaiming (his) omniscience and his association with divinities, make his own side exultant and the enemy's side alarmed.
10.03.34 | (Thinking) "tomorrow is the battle", he, having kept a fast, should lie down beside his weapons and vehicle.
10.03.35 | And he should make offerings with Atharvan (spells).
10.03.36 | And he should have benedictions pronounced that are connected with victory and relate to heaven.
10.03.37 | And he should commend himself to the brahmins.
10.03.38 | He should make the core of the army (one) endowed with valour, skill, noble birth and loyalty, and not let down in money and honour.
10.03.39 | A bare army without banners, of soldiers who have their fathers, sons and brothers (with them), is the king's station.
10.03.40 | An elephant or a chariot, with a following of horses, is the king's vehicle.
10.03.41 | Whichever (animal) his army mostly consists of, or on whichever he is trained, that he should mount.
10.03.42 | A man bearing the marks of the king is to be posted at the station of the battle array.
10.03.43 | Bards and minstrels should describe heaven for the brave and the absence of heaven for cowards, and the praise of the caste, corporation, family, actions and conduct of the fighters.
10.03.44 | The chaplain's men should speak of witchcraft and sorcery (performed against the enemy); the engineers, carpenters and astrologers (should speak of) the success of their own work and the failure of the enemies'.
10.03.45 | The commander of the army should address the army, well treated with wealth and honour: "The killing of the king is worth a hundred thousand; the killing of the commander or a prince, fifty thousand; the killing of a chief of the champions, ten thousand; the killing of an elephant or a chariot (warrior), five thousand; the killing of a horse(man), a thousand; the killing of a chief of foot-soldiers, a hundred; a head, twenty; and doubling of pay and whatever one seizes oneself."
10.03.46 | The chiefs of groups of ten should know this about them.
10.03.47 | Physicians with surgical instruments, appliances, antidotes, oils and bandages in their hands, and women guarding food and drink and serving to encourage the men, should stand at the rear.
10.03.48 | He should array the army on his own ground so that it does not face south, has the sun at its back and the wind favourable.
10.03.49 | And when arraying on the enemy's ground, they should make the horses move about.
10.03.50 | Where the ground is unsuitable for the array both for standing firm and for rapid advance, there, whether standing or rushing forward, he would be defeated in both ways.
10.03.51 | In the opposite case he wins, in both ways, in standing and in rapid advance.
10.03.52 | The ground, whether even, uneven or mixed, is to be known in front, on the two flanks and at the rear.
10.03.53 | On even ground, the staff and circle arrays; on uneven ground, the snake and the loose arrays; on mixed ground, irregular arrays.
10.03.54 | Having broken (an enemy) of superior strength, he should sue for peace.
10.03.55 | Asked by one of equal strength, he should make peace.
10.03.56 | He should pursue and strike an inferior (enemy), but never one who has reached his own ground or one who has given up his life (resolved to die).
10.03.57a | In one who turns back again and has despaired of life there arises an irresistible impetus; therefore one should not press a broken (enemy).

10.04.1 | For foot-soldiers, horses, chariots and elephants, their own ground is desired in battle and in encampment.
10.04.2 | For men who fight in the desert, in forests, on low ground and on high dry ground, and for those who fight in trenches, in the open, by day and by night, and for elephants and horses from rivers, mountains, marshes and lakes, battle-grounds and times are desired each according to its own.
10.04.3 | Ground for chariots is one that is level, firm and open, free from pits, not hurting wheels and hooves, not catching the axle, not made impassable by trees, shrubs, creepers, stumps, fields, holes, anthills, sand and mud, and free from crevices; for elephants and horses, and for men, ground both even and uneven is suitable in battle and in encampment.
10.04.4 | Ground for horses is one with small stones and trees, with small holes that can be leapt over, and with a slight defect of crevices.
10.04.5 | Ground for foot-soldiers is one with big stumps, stones, trees, creepers, anthills and shrubs.
10.04.6 | Ground for elephants is one with hills, low ground and uneven places that can be traversed, with trees that can be trampled down, with creepers that can be cut, with mud that can be broken through, and free from crevices.
10.04.7 | Ground free from thorns, not very uneven, and provided with a place of retreat: this is the special advantage for foot-soldiers.
10.04.8 | Ground with twice the place of retreat, free from mud, water and whatever causes lameness, and without gravel: this is the special advantage for horses.
10.04.9 | Ground containing dust, mud, water, reeds and rushes, free from dog's-tooth thorns, and free from the striking of branches of big trees: this is the special advantage for elephants.
10.04.10 | Ground having water-reservoirs and places of shelter, free from pits, free from fields, and allowing (chariots) to turn about: this is the special advantage for chariots.
10.04.11 | The ground for all (the arms) has been stated.
10.04.12 | By this, the encampments of all troops and the battles stand explained.
10.04.13 | Searching out ground, camping sites and forests; securing favourable water, fords, wind and sunlight; destroying or protecting supply-lines and reinforcements; clearing and steadying the army; extending the range of foraging; driving off (the enemy) on the flanks; the first strike; penetrating (the enemy ranks); piercing through; rallying; capturing; releasing; changing the line of pursuit; carrying off the treasury and the princes; striking at the rear and the wings; pursuing the weakened; following up; and acting as a concentrated body: these are the functions of the cavalry.
10.04.14 | Marching in front; making roads, camps and fords where there are none; driving off (the enemy) on the flanks; crossing water and descending into it; halting, marching and descending; entering uneven and narrow places; setting and quenching fire; victory by a single arm (of the army); reuniting broken (ranks); breaking unbroken (ranks); protection in calamity; striking; terrifying; frightening; imposing grandeur; capturing; releasing; breaking ramparts, gates and towers; and carrying away the treasury and transport: these are the functions of elephants.
10.04.15 | Protecting one's own army; repelling the (enemy's) fourfold army; capturing and releasing in battle; reuniting broken (ranks); breaking unbroken (ranks); frightening; imposing grandeur; and a terrible noise: these are the functions of chariots.
10.04.16 | Carrying weapons in all places and at all times, and exertion (in fighting): these are the functions of foot-soldiers.
10.04.17 | The work of clearing camps, roads, bridges, wells and fords; carrying machines, weapons, armour, implements and food; and removing weapons, armour and the wounded from the battlefield: these are the functions of the labour corps (viṣṭi).

10.05.1 | Having set up the fort at a distance of five hundred bows, he should go to battle; or according to the lie of the ground.
10.05.2 | The commander of the army and the leader should array the army, with its chiefs assigned to separate units, after moving it out in a place out of sight (of the enemy).
10.05.3 | He should station a foot-soldier at an interval of one śama, a horse at an interval of three śamas, a chariot or an elephant at an interval of five śamas.
10.05.4 | Or he should array (them) with double or triple the interval.
10.05.5 | Thus one would fight at ease, without crowding.
10.05.6 | A bow (the measure) is five aratnis (cubits).
10.05.7 | In that (space of one bow) he should station an archer, a horse in three bows, a chariot or an elephant in five bows.
10.05.8 | The junction between the divisions of the wings, the flanks and the centre (breast) is five bows.
10.05.9 | For a horse, three men are the fighters in front.
10.05.10 | Fifteen for a chariot or an elephant, and five horses.
10.05.11 | As many foot-guards should be assigned for the horses, chariots and elephants.
10.05.12 | He should station a division of three triads of chariots as the centre, and as many as flank and wing on both sides.
10.05.13 | Thus in the chariot array there are forty-five chariots, two hundred and twenty-five horses, six hundred and seventy-five men as fighters in front, and as many foot-guards.
10.05.14 | This is the even array (sama-vyūha).
10.05.15 | Its increase is by two chariots each time, up to twenty-one chariots.
10.05.16 | Thus there are ten odd-numbered basic forms of the even array.
10.05.17 | When the wings, flanks and centre are unequal in number among themselves, it is the uneven array (viṣama-vyūha).
10.05.18 | Its increase too is by two chariots each time, up to twenty-one chariots.
10.05.19 | Thus there are ten odd-numbered basic forms of the uneven array.
10.05.20 | After this, the remainder of the troops beyond the array should be made the āvāpa (the surplus addition).
10.05.21 | He should add two-thirds of the chariots as surplus in the limbs (wings and flanks); the rest he should station in the centre.
10.05.22 | Thus the surplus of chariots should be made less by one third.
10.05.23 | By that the surplus of elephants and of horses is explained.
10.05.24 | The surplus should be made (only) so long as it does not cause crowding in battle for the horses, chariots and elephants.
10.05.25 | A preponderance of the army is āvāpa (surplus addition).
10.05.26 | A preponderance of infantry is pratyāvāpa (counter-surplus).
10.05.27 | A preponderance of one arm (of the army) is anvāvāpa (after-surplus).
10.05.28 | A preponderance of the treasonable (troops) is atyāvāpa (excess-surplus).
10.05.29 | The surplus of troops should be from fourfold up to eightfold compared with the enemy's surplus and counter-surplus, or according to one's resources.
10.05.30 | By the chariot array the elephant array is explained.
10.05.31 | Or a mixed (array) of elephants, chariots and horses: elephants at the extremities, horses on the two sides, chariots in the centre.
10.05.32 | The centre of elephants, the two flanks of chariots, the two wings of horses: this is the 'breaker of the middle' (madhya-bhedī).
10.05.33 | The reverse (arrangement) is the 'breaker of the ends' (anta-bhedī).
10.05.34 | But the pure (array) of elephants alone: the centre of armoured elephants, the rear of riding elephants, the two tips of vicious elephants.
10.05.35 | The horse array: the centre of armoured (horses), the flank and wing of plain (unarmoured) ones.
10.05.36 | The infantry array: in front the shield-bearers, behind the archers.
10.05.37 | These are the pure (unmixed) arrays.
10.05.38 | Foot-soldiers on the two wings, horses on the two sides, elephants behind, chariots in front; or a reversal according to the enemy's array.
10.05.39 | This is the division of forces with two arms.
10.05.40 | By that the division of forces with three arms is explained.
10.05.41 | The excellence of the army is a strong (picked) force of men.
10.05.42 | The distinction (excellence) of elephants and horses consists in: breed, stock, spirit, being in the prime of age, vitality, size, speed, mettle, training, steadiness, tall stature, docility, and good marks together with good behaviour.
10.05.43 | Of the foot-soldiers, horses, chariots and elephants he should station one third of the strong (troops) in the centre, two thirds in the flank and wing on both sides; the next-best in the same order, the third-best in reverse order, the weak in reverse order.
10.05.44 | Thus he should bring everything into use.
10.05.45 | Having placed the weak force at the ends, it becomes one that makes a swift assault.
10.05.46 | Having placed the strong force in front, he should put the next-best at the tips, the third-best at the rear, and the weak force in the middle.
10.05.47 | Thus this (array) becomes able to endure (an attack).
10.05.48 | But having set up the array, he should strike with one or two of the wings, flanks and centre, and with the rest give support (hold in reserve).
10.05.49 | Whatever (part) of the enemy is weak, devoid of elephants and horses, has treasonable ministers, or has had secret instigation made in it, that he should strike with strong troops exceeding it.
10.05.50 | Or whatever (part) of the enemy is the strongest, that he should strike with strong troops twice that.
10.05.51 | Whichever arm of his own has little strength, that he should reinforce with many (troops).
10.05.52 | From which side the enemy's loss (would come), on that side he should array near by; or from which side there would be danger.
10.05.53 | Charging forward, riding around, riding past, withdrawing, crushing and holding, the ring, the zigzag (go-mūtrikā), the circle, the scattered attack (prakīrṇikā), turning the back, following in column, protection of the broken troops from the front, from the two sides and from behind, pursuit of the broken troops: these are the modes of cavalry fighting.
10.05.54 | These same, excluding the scattered attack, the destruction of the four arms separately or combined, and the smashing of the wings, flanks and centre, assault, and the night attack on the sleeping: these are the modes of elephant fighting.
10.05.55 | These same, excluding crushing and holding, and fighting while advancing, retreating and standing on one's own ground: these are the modes of chariot fighting.
10.05.56 | Striking in all places and at all times, and secret punishment (upāṃśu-daṇḍa): these are the modes of infantry fighting.
10.05.57a | By this method he should have arrays made, odd and even, so long as the resources of the four arms would be adequate to them.

10.06.1 | Two wings, the centre and the reserve: this is the division of the battle array according to the school of Usanas.
10.06.2 | Two wings, two flanks, the centre and the reserve: this is (the division) according to the school of Brihaspati.
10.06.3 | The fore-wings, flanks and centre (are) of both; the staff (danda), the snake (bhoga), the circle (mandala) and the loose array (asamhata) are the basic arrays.
10.06.4 | Among these, the one whose disposition is transverse is the staff.
10.06.5 | The movement of all (the parts) one following the other is the snake.
10.06.6 | The movement in all directions of those that move is the circle.
10.06.7 | The disposition of those standing (in position) in separate divisions is the loose array.
10.06.8 | The (array) standing evenly with wings, flanks and centre is the staff.
10.06.9 | That same (staff), with the flanks advanced forward, is the pradara (the breaker).
10.06.10 | That same, drawn back with the wings and flanks, is the dridhaka (the firm).
10.06.11 | That same, advanced with the wings, is the asahya (the irresistible).
10.06.12 | Keeping the wings in place, (the array) with the centre advanced is the shyena (the hawk).
10.06.13 | In the reverse (of these four) they are the chapa (bow), the chapakukshi (bow's belly), the pratishtha (steady) and the supratishtha (very steady).
10.06.14 | The (array) whose wings are bows is the sanjaya (the victorious).
10.06.15 | That same, with the centre advanced, is the vijaya (victory).
10.06.16 | The (array) whose wings have thick ears (projecting ends) is the sthunakarna (post-eared).
10.06.17 | The (array) with the posts of the wings doubled is the vishalavijaya (broad victory).
10.06.18 | The (array) whose wings are advanced three times over is the chamumukha (army-face).
10.06.19 | In the reverse (of that) it is the jhashasya (fish-mouth).
10.06.20 | The staff (drawn up) in a straight lengthwise line is the suchi (needle).
10.06.21 | Two staffs are the valaya (bracelet).
10.06.22 | Four (staffs) are the durjaya (hard to conquer).
10.06.23 | These are the staff arrays.
10.06.24 | The (array) standing unevenly with wings, flanks and centre is the snake.
10.06.25 | That is the sarpasari (moving like a snake) or the gomutrika (the zigzag of a cow's urine).
10.06.26 | That same, with a double centre and staff-like wings, is the shakata (cart).
10.06.27 | In the reverse (of that) it is the makara (crocodile).
10.06.28 | The cart intermixed with elephants, horses and chariots is the paripatantaka (the one flying about).
10.06.29 | These are the snake arrays.
10.06.30 | When the wings, flanks and centre become one, it is the circle.
10.06.31 | That, facing in all directions, is the sarvatobhadra (good on all sides).
10.06.32 | (The circle) with eight divisions is the durjaya (hard to conquer).
10.06.33 | These are the circle arrays.
10.06.34 | From the wings, flanks and centre not being joined together, it is the loose array.
10.06.35 | That, by placing five divisions in the (corresponding) shape, is the vajra (thunderbolt) or the godha (iguana).
10.06.36 | (The arrangement) of four (divisions) is the uddhanaka or the kakapadi (crow's foot).
10.06.37 | (The arrangement) of three (divisions) is the ardhachandraka (half-moon) or the karkatakashringi (crab's horns).
10.06.38 | These are the loose arrays.
10.06.39 | The (array) with chariots in the centre, elephants in the flanks and horses at the rear is the arishta (the unharmed).
10.06.40 | Foot-soldiers, horses, chariots and elephants, one behind the other: the achala (the immovable).
10.06.41 | Elephants, horses, chariots and foot-soldiers, one behind the other: the apratihata (the unopposed).
10.06.42 | Of these, he should strike down the pradara with the dridhaka, the dridhaka with the asahya, the shyena with the chapa, the pratishtha with the supratishtha, the sanjaya with the vijaya, the sthunakarna with the vishalavijaya, and the paripatantaka with the sarvatobhadra.
10.06.43 | With the durjaya he should array (his forces) against all of them.
10.06.44 | Of foot-soldiers, horses, chariots and elephants, he should strike down each earlier one with the one following it, and (a force) deficient in a limb with (one) having a limb in excess.
10.06.45 | Of a group of ten units, one chief is the patika; of a group of ten patikas, one is the senapati (army commander); of a group of ten of those, one is the nayaka (leader) — thus.
10.06.46 | He should establish signals for the parts of the array by means of musical instruments, shouts, standards and flags, for separating the parts, massing them together, halting, marching, wheeling round and attacking.
10.06.47 | When the array (on both sides) is equal, success comes from the (right) use of place, time and strength.
