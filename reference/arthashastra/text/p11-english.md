11.01.1 | The gaining of a saṅgha (a confederacy) is the best among the gains of army and ally.
11.01.2 | For saṅghas, because they are united, are unassailable by enemies.
11.01.3 | He should make use of those that are favourable by means of conciliation and gifts, and of those that are hostile by means of dissension and force.
11.01.4 | The Kambojas, the Surashtras, the kshatriya guilds and others live by economic activity (vārttā) and by arms.
11.01.5 | The Licchivikas, the Vrijikas, the Mallakas, the Madrakas, the Kukuras, the Kurus, the Panchalas and others live by the title of king.
11.01.6 | Secret agents stationed near all the saṅghas, having found out the occasions of mutual slight, hatred, enmity and quarrel, should set in motion dissension brought about step by step: 'That one speaks ill of you.'
11.01.7 | Thus agents in the guise of teachers should stir up quarrels, in matters of learning, crafts, gaming and amusements, among the young of those whose anger has been roused on both sides.
11.01.8 | Or in brothels and taverns, assassins (tīkṣṇas) should stir up quarrels among the men of the chiefs of the saṅghas by praising the opposite side, or by winning over the seducible party.
11.01.9 | They should incite the young men who get an inferior share by means of the superior share.
11.01.10 | And they should prevent the inferior from sharing a common dish or marriage with the superior.
11.01.11 | Or they should get the inferior to share a common dish or marriage with the superior.
11.01.12 | Or they should set the inferior on claiming equal status on grounds of family, of valour, or of a reversal of rank.
11.01.13 | Or they should bring an established practice to an end by setting up the reverse of it.
11.01.14 | Or, in matters under dispute, assassins should stir up quarrels at night by striking at goods, cattle or men.
11.01.15 | And on all occasions of quarrel the king, having supported the weaker party with treasury and army, should set it to the destruction of the opposing party.
11.01.16 | Or he should remove elsewhere those who have been split off.
11.01.17 | And he should settle them on cultivable land in groups of five or ten families.
11.01.18 | For, staying in one place, they might become capable of taking up arms.
11.01.19 | And he should lay down a penalty for their assembling together.
11.01.20 | He should install in the status of a prince one of good family and noble birth who has been kept out or cast down by those bearing the title of king.
11.01.21 | And his group, the soothsayer and others, should proclaim in the saṅghas his possession of the marks of a king.
11.01.22 | And he should secretly instigate the most righteous chiefs of the saṅghas: 'Fulfil your own dharma towards the son or the brother of that king.'
11.01.23 | When they have agreed, he should send money and an army for the purpose of winning over the seducible party.
11.01.24 | At the time of the attack, agents in the guise of liquor-sellers, on the pretext of the death of a son or wife, saying 'this is the funeral offering', should give out by hundreds jars of liquor mixed with madana juice.
11.01.25 | And at sanctuaries, shrines, gates and guard-posts, secret agents should point out a deposit relating to an act of covenant, tokens and seals together with gold, and vessels of gold.
11.01.26 | And when the saṅghas are seen (there), they should report: 'They are the king's men.'
11.01.27 | Then he should make an assault.
11.01.28 | Or, having taken on loan for a time conveyances and gold from the saṅghas, he should give the chief of the saṅgha well-known goods.
11.01.29 | When these ask for it back, he should say: 'It has been given to that chief.'
11.01.30 | By this is explained the sowing of dissension in a military camp and among forest tribes.
11.01.31 | Or a secret agent should make the son of a chief of a saṅgha, who thinks highly of himself, believe: 'You are the son of that king; from fear of enemies you have been placed here.'
11.01.32 | The king, having supported with treasury and army the one who has accepted this, should make him attack the saṅghas.
11.01.33 | Having attained his object, he should do away with him too.
11.01.34 | Keepers of harlots, or acrobats, actors, dancers and showmen, sent out as agents, should infatuate the chiefs of the saṅghas with women of supreme beauty and youth.
11.01.35 | Having created confidence in one of those in whom desire has arisen, they should stir up quarrels by (her) going elsewhere or by forcible abduction.
11.01.36 | In the quarrel the assassins should do the deed, so that it is said: 'This lover has been killed thus.'
11.01.37 | Or, approaching one who has been cheated and yet bears it patiently, the woman should say: 'That chief harasses me, who am in love with you; while he lives I will not stay here' — and so she should bring about his killing.
11.01.38 | Or, when carried off by force, she should have the abductor killed by an assassin at night at the edge of a forest or in a pleasure-house, or (do so) herself with poison.
11.01.39 | Then she should proclaim: 'My beloved has been killed by that man.'
11.01.40 | Or an agent in the guise of a holy man (siddha), having brought one in whom desire has arisen to cohabit by means of love-winning herbs, should overreach him with poison and go away.
11.01.41 | When he has gone away, secret agents should allege that it was the doing of an enemy.
11.01.42 | Women agents, rich widows with a secret livelihood, disputing over an inheritance or a deposit, should infatuate the chiefs of the saṅghas; or women of the Aditi-Kausika (class), or dancing-girls and singers.
11.01.43 | Those who have responded and entered secret houses for a nocturnal rendezvous, assassins should kill, or bind and carry off.
11.01.44 | Or a secret agent should tell a chief of a saṅgha who is greedy for women: 'In that village a poor family has withdrawn; the woman of that family is worthy of a king; take her.'
11.01.45 | When she has been taken, after half a month the agent in the guise of a holy man should cry out in the midst of the treasonable chiefs of the saṅgha: 'That chief is violating my wife, or daughter-in-law, or sister, or daughter.'
11.01.46 | If the saṅgha punishes him, the king, having taken him under his protection, should make him attack the hostile ones.
11.01.47 | If he is not punished, assassins should do away with the agent in the guise of a holy man at night.
11.01.48 | Then agents in that same guise should cry out: 'That man is a slayer of a brahman and the paramour of a brahman's wife.'
11.01.49 | Or an agent in the guise of a soothsayer should describe to one man a maiden betrothed to another: 'The daughter of that man will become a king's wife and the mother of a king; obtain her with all your property or by force.'
11.01.50 | If she is not obtained, he should incite the other party.
11.01.51 | If she is obtained, the quarrel is accomplished.
11.01.52 | Or a female mendicant should say to a chief who is fond of his wife: 'That chief, puffed up with youth, sent me to your wife; out of fear of him I have come, bringing the letter and the ornament; your wife is blameless; revenge must be taken on him secretly; I too, for my part, will act accordingly.'
11.01.53 | On these and similar occasions of quarrel, whether the quarrel has arisen of itself or has been produced by assassins, the king, having supported the weaker party with treasury and army, should make it attack the hostile ones, or should remove it elsewhere.
11.01.54 | Thus should a sole king (monarch) conduct himself towards the saṅghas.
11.01.55 | And the saṅghas too should in the same way guard themselves against these devices of overreaching on the part of a sole king.
11.01.56a | And a chief of a saṅgha should remain among the saṅghas of just conduct, beneficial, agreeable, self-controlled, with devoted people, complying with the minds of all.
